# X | Exam skills | How HL Economics is assessed and how to write answers that reach the top markbands: command terms, the three papers, the IA and evaluation.

## x-assessment | Assessment at a glance | SL | The components, weightings, timings and assessment objectives for HL (and SL).

### assessment | Components and weightings
@terms
Assessment objectives (AOs) :: The four skills IB Economics assesses: AO1 knowledge and understanding, AO2 application and analysis, AO3 synthesis and evaluation, AO4 use of skills (diagrams, calculations, data).
Internal assessment (IA) :: The coursework component: a portfolio of three commentaries on news articles, marked by your teacher and moderated by the IB.
@end
| Component | HL | SL | What it is |
| **Paper 1** | 1h 15m · 25 marks · **20%** | 1h 15m · 25 marks · 30% | Extended response: choose **one** of three questions; part (a) 10 marks, part (b) 15 marks. No calculator |
| **Paper 2** | 1h 45m · 40 marks · **30%** | 1h 45m · 40 marks · 40% | Data response: choose **one** of two questions, parts (a)–(g); the same paper for SL and HL since May 2024, so no HL-only content |
| **Paper 3** | 1h 45m · 60 marks · **30%** | — | HL only. Policy paper: **two compulsory** questions, each with short quantitative and diagram tasks and a 10-mark policy recommendation. Calculator allowed |
| **IA** | 45 marks · **20%** | 45 marks · 30% | Portfolio of three 800-word commentaries |

:::tip
Paper 3 and Paper 2 together are 60% of the HL grade, and both reward **calculations and use of data**. Drill the calculations in [[formulas|Formulas]] until they are automatic.
:::

#### The four assessment objectives
| AO | Skill | Typical command terms |
| **AO1** | Knowledge and understanding: definitions, theory, terminology | Define, describe, list, outline, state |
| **AO2** | Application and analysis: using theory in context and in data | Analyse, apply, comment, distinguish, explain, suggest |
| **AO3** | Synthesis and evaluation: weighing arguments to reach a judgement | Compare and contrast, discuss, evaluate, examine, justify, recommend, to what extent |
| **AO4** | Skills: diagrams, calculations, data | Calculate, construct, determine, draw, identify, label, plot, show, sketch |

### command-terms | Command terms
@terms
Define :: Give the precise meaning of a word, phrase, concept or physical quantity.
Describe :: Give a detailed account.
Outline :: Give a brief account or summary.
State :: Give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation.
Explain :: Give a detailed account including reasons or causes.
Analyse :: Break down to bring out the essential elements or structure; show cause-and-effect relationships.
Distinguish :: Make clear the differences between two or more concepts or items.
Comment :: Give a judgement based on a given statement or result of a calculation.
Suggest :: Propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer.
Discuss :: Offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses, with conclusions supported by evidence.
Evaluate :: Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations.
Examine :: Consider an argument or concept in a way that uncovers the assumptions and interrelationships of the issue.
To what extent :: Consider the merits or otherwise of an argument or concept, with opinions and conclusions clearly presented and supported with appropriate evidence and sound argument.
Justify :: Give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion.
Recommend :: Present an advisable course of action with appropriate supporting evidence or reasons in relation to a given situation, problem or issue.
Compare and contrast :: Give an account of similarities and differences between two or more items, referring to both of them throughout.
Calculate :: Obtain a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working.
Draw :: Represent by means of a labelled, accurate diagram or graph, using a pencil and ruler where needed.
Sketch :: Represent by means of a diagram or graph (labelled as appropriate), giving a general idea of the required shape or relationship.
Construct :: Display information in a diagrammatic or logical form.
Identify :: Provide an answer from a number of possibilities.
@end
The command term tells you **how much to write** and **which skill earns the marks**. "Explain" (AO2) never needs evaluation; "evaluate" (AO3) always needs a balanced judgement. All definitions above follow the IB's glossary of command terms (paraphrased).

:::tip
Underline the command term and the **variable** in every question. "Explain how a subsidy affects **producers**" is a different answer from "…affects **the market**".
:::

## x-paper1 | Paper 1: extended response | SL | How to plan and write the 10-mark and 15-mark essays.

### p1-structure | Paper 1: what is tested
@terms
Real-world example :: A specific, accurate case (country, market, policy, firm, date) used to support analysis and evaluation.
@end
- Choose **one** question from three (micro, macro and global topics are all possible). Each has **(a) 10 marks** and **(b) 15 marks**. 75 minutes total: about **25 minutes for (a), 45–50 for (b)**, 5 minutes reading and planning.
- No data or case study is given; you supply the theory, diagrams and real-world examples.
- Memorised essays that do not answer the specific question are penalised.

#### What the markbands reward (summary)
| Part | Top band needs |
| **(a) 10 marks** | The question answered **directly**; relevant terms **defined**; **accurate, fully labelled diagram(s) explained** in the text; clear chain of reasoning. **No evaluation and no real-world example required** |
| **(b) 15 marks** | All of the above, plus **effective, specific real-world example(s)** and **synthesis and evaluation**: balanced arguments, supported by theory and evidence, leading to a **justified judgement** |

### p1a | Writing part (a): the 10-mark explanation
@terms
@end
**Structure (DDE)**:
1. **Define** the key terms in the question (one sentence each, precise).
2. **Diagram**: draw it accurately with full labels, a title, arrows for shifts, and equilibrium points.
3. **Explain** the diagram step by step: "The tax shifts supply from S to S + tax, so price rises from P₁ to Pc and quantity falls from Q₁ to Q₂…". Refer to labels on the diagram in your text.
4. **Cover the full scope**: if the question says "the market", cover consumers, producers and government; if it says "two ways", give two developed ways.
5. Stop. No conclusion and no evaluation needed.

:::model Plan: "Explain how an increase in the minimum wage might affect the labour market" [10]
- Define minimum wage, labour market, unemployment.
- Diagram: labour market (Wage / Quantity of labour), SL and DL, Wmin above We, Ld and Ls; unemployment = Ls − Ld.
- Explain: higher wage raises firms' costs → quantity of labour demanded contracts (movement along DL); more people willing to work → quantity supplied extends; surplus of labour = unemployment.
- Explain the size depends on wage elasticity of demand for labour (inelastic → small fall in jobs).
- Optional second point: higher incomes for workers who keep their jobs, higher consumption.
:::

### p1b | Writing part (b): the 15-mark evaluation
@terms
@end
**Structure**:
1. **Introduction**: define terms; state the argument you will test.
2. **Theory and diagram**: explain the mechanism (often one main diagram, sometimes two).
3. **Real-world example(s)**: specific and integrated (country, policy, date, what happened, numbers if you know them). Use it as evidence for analysis, not a story.
4. **Evaluation**: at least two or three developed points on each side, using the frameworks in [[e-evaluation|the evaluation toolkit]].
5. **Conclusion**: a **justified judgement** that answers the question, with conditions: "The tax is likely to be effective *if* demand is elastic and revenue is used to…".

:::model Plan: "Using real-world examples, evaluate the impact of a price floor in an agricultural market" [15]
- Define price floor; diagram of price floor with surplus and government purchase (Pmin × surplus).
- Example: EU Common Agricultural Policy intervention buying in the 1980s ("butter mountains"), or India's minimum support prices for wheat and rice.
- For: stable, higher farm incomes; food security; protects rural employment.
- Against: surplus and storage costs (opportunity cost); higher prices for consumers (regressive); inefficient farms protected; dumping hurts farmers abroad; overallocation.
- Stakeholders: farmers gain, consumers and taxpayers lose, foreign producers lose.
- Judgement: justified only as a temporary, targeted safety net; direct income support or buffer stocks cause fewer distortions (the EU moved to decoupled payments after 2003).
:::

:::warn
- Evaluation that is just a list of disadvantages: weigh them and **reach a judgement**.
- Vague examples ("many countries use taxes"). Name the country, the policy and the result.
- Unlabelled or unexplained diagrams.
- Not answering the exact question (e.g. writing about subsidies in general when the question asks about their effect on equity).
:::

## x-paper2 | Paper 2: data response | SL | Short-answer technique, using the text and data, and the 15-mark part (g).

### p2 | Paper 2: technique for parts (a) to (g)
@terms
@end
- **Choose one of two questions.** Each has text extracts, tables and charts on a real country or market. Total 40 marks in 1h 45m.
- Parts (a)–(f): definitions (2 marks), calculations (1–3 marks), diagrams with explanation (4 marks), short explanations linked to the text (4 marks).
- Part (g): **15 marks**, a data-driven evaluation, e.g. "Using the text/data and your knowledge of economics, evaluate the effectiveness of…".

#### Short answers
| Marks | What to write |
| **2-mark define** | One precise definition, with both key elements (e.g. inflation: a **sustained** rise in the **average price level**). No example needed |
| **2-mark list/state** | Two distinct, correct points; one line each |
| **Calculations** | Formula, substitution, answer with **units**, 2 d.p. Show working: method marks |
| **4-mark explain with diagram** | Correct, fully labelled diagram + explanation that refers to it and to the context. A wrong or poorly labelled diagram can cost half the marks |
| **4-mark explain (no diagram)** | One or two short paragraphs with a clear causal chain, linked to the text |

#### Part (g): the 15-mark data question
- Same skills as Paper 1 (b), but evaluation **must use the text and data**. Theory without reference to the stimulus is capped.
- Quote figures ("unemployment fell from 7.2% to 5.1% (Table 2)"), refer to paragraphs ("Text C states…"), and link each argument to the country's context.
- Structure: brief definitions → theory (often with a diagram) → 2–3 arguments for, 2–3 against, each anchored in the data → judgement grounded in the evidence.
- **Time**: leave at least 35 minutes for (g). Overspending on early parts is the most common reason for a weak (g).

:::tip
Before writing part (g), list every piece of data in the extracts that is relevant. Use at least four or five specific references.
:::

## x-paper3 | Paper 3: HL policy paper | HL | Quantitative technique and how to write the 10-mark policy recommendation.

### p3 | Paper 3: technique | HL
@terms
Policy recommendation :: A justified proposal of one policy to address an economic problem identified in the data.
@end
- **Two compulsory questions**, 60 marks, 1h 45m, calculator allowed. Each question presents data on a country or market.
- **Part (a)**: many short tasks (1–4 marks): calculations (elasticities, tax revenue, welfare areas, multiplier, current account, exchange rates, index numbers, Lorenz curves), constructing and labelling diagrams, definitions and short explanations.
- **Part (b)**: a **10-mark policy recommendation** using the data and your answers to part (a).

#### Calculation rules
1. Write the **formula** first.
2. Substitute values from the data, showing every step.
3. Give **units** ($, %, units, $bn) and round to **2 decimal places** unless told otherwise; do not round intermediate steps.
4. Reuse answers from earlier parts: if you got one wrong, follow-through marks are still possible.
5. Comment on the meaning of your result if asked ("PED = −0.4, so demand is price inelastic…").

#### The 10-mark policy recommendation
1. **Identify the problem** using the data (e.g. a current account deficit of 6% of GDP, or youth unemployment of 28%).
2. **Recommend one policy**. Only one: listing several policies weakens depth.
3. **Explain how it works**, with theory and (if useful) a diagram.
4. **Use the data and your part (a) results** throughout ("the multiplier of 2.5 calculated in (a)(iv) means a $4bn spending increase would close the $10bn output gap").
5. **Evaluate**: costs, trade-offs, time lags, stakeholders, feasibility given the country's constraints (debt level, inflation, exchange rate system).
6. **Conclude** with a justified recommendation.

:::model Plan: recommend a policy to reduce a persistent current account deficit
Problem from data: CA deficit −5.8% of GDP; inflation 2%; unemployment 4%; floating currency. Policy: supply-side investment in export-sector skills and infrastructure (or allow depreciation). How: raises productivity and competitiveness → exports rise → CA improves; diagram LRAS shift. Data use: exports are 70% primary commodities (vulnerable), PEDs from (a) sum to 1.3 (so a depreciation would work, but J-curve). Evaluation: long lag; cost vs debt of 85% of GDP; alternative depreciation would raise import prices. Judgement: combine a managed depreciation now with supply-side measures; recommend supply-side as the main policy because it tackles the cause.
:::

## x-ia | Internal assessment | SL | The portfolio of three commentaries: rules, criteria and how to score well.

### ia | The IA portfolio
@terms
Commentary :: An 800-word maximum analysis of a news article, applying economic theory and a key concept to the event described.
@end
#### Rules
- **Three commentaries**, each based on a **different unit** of the syllabus (Unit 2, 3 or 4; **not** Unit 1).
- Each uses a **different key concept** as its lens (scarcity, choice, efficiency, equity, economic well-being, sustainability, change, interdependence, intervention).
- Each article from a **different, appropriate source** (news media), published **no earlier than one year** before you write the commentary.
- **Maximum 800 words** each (excluding diagrams, labels, tables, citations, headings).
- Include the article (or extract) and the required **cover sheet** (title, source, date, word count, unit, key concept).

#### Criteria (each commentary out of 14, plus 3 for the rubric)
| Criterion | Marks | Top level needs |
| **A: Diagrams** | 3 | Relevant, accurate, labelled diagrams with a full explanation linked to the article |
| **B: Terminology** | 2 | Relevant economic terms used accurately throughout |
| **C: Application and analysis** | 3 | Theory applied effectively to the article's situation, with analysis |
| **D: Key concept** | 3 | The chosen key concept is identified, explained and linked to the article throughout |
| **E: Evaluation** | 3 | Effective, balanced evaluation with judgements supported by reasoning |
| **F/G/H: Rubric requirements** (whole portfolio) | 3 | All rules met: different units, concepts, sources; dates; word limits |
| **Total** | 3 × 14 + 3 = **45** | |

#### Writing a strong commentary
1. Choose a **narrow** article about a specific event (a new tax, a central bank rate decision, a tariff), not a general opinion piece.
2. Open by identifying the economic issue and defining terms.
3. Draw **one or two** diagrams tailored to the article (label with real values or names: "Price of Indonesian palm oil").
4. Analyse using the article's facts.
5. Link explicitly to the **key concept** (e.g. "this tariff illustrates **interdependence** because…").
6. Evaluate: stakeholders, short vs long run, alternative policies, assumptions, a judgement.

## x-evaluation | Evaluation toolkit and real-world examples | SL | Ready-made frameworks for evaluation and a bank of examples by topic.

### evaluation | The evaluation toolkit
@terms
Unintended consequences :: Effects of a policy that were not intended, such as black markets after a price ceiling.
Government failure :: When government intervention leads to a less efficient allocation of resources than before.
@end
Use these lenses to generate evaluation for any 15-mark answer:

| Lens | Questions to ask |
| **Short run vs long run** | Does the effect grow or fade? (PED and PES rise over time; supply-side policy works slowly) |
| **Stakeholders** | Who gains and who loses: consumers, producers, workers, government, foreign firms, future generations? |
| **Magnitude** | How big is the change? Depends on PED, PES, the multiplier, the size of the tax or subsidy |
| **Assumptions** | Rational behaviour? Perfect information? Ceteris paribus? Are they realistic here? |
| **Priorities** | Efficiency vs equity; growth vs sustainability; inflation vs unemployment |
| **Context** | Developed or developing economy? Debt level? Exchange rate system? Institutions? |
| **Alternatives** | Is another policy cheaper, faster or fairer? |
| **Unintended consequences and government failure** | Black markets, regulatory capture, information problems, political short-termism |
| **Measurement** | Can the externality, the target or the effect be measured reliably? |
| **Opportunity cost** | What else could the money or resources have been used for? |

:::tip
Make evaluation **specific**: "Because the PED for cigarettes is around −0.4, a 20% tax-driven price rise would cut consumption by only about 8%, so revenue rises but the externality falls little" beats "it depends on elasticity".
:::

## x-diagram-skills | Diagram skills | SL | Rules for drawing exam diagrams and the must-know list.

### diagram-rules | Rules for exam diagrams
@terms
@end
1. **Use a ruler and pencil**; draw large (at least a third of a page).
2. **Title** every diagram (e.g. "Market for cigarettes in Australia").
3. **Label both axes** correctly: Price and Quantity for micro; Price level and Real GDP for macro; Exchange rate and Quantity of currency for forex; Inflation and Unemployment for Phillips curves.
4. **Label every curve** (D, S, MSC, MPB, AD, SRAS, LRAS…) and every **equilibrium** (P₁, Q₁, E₁).
5. **Show changes** with arrows and subscripts (D₁ → D₂), and use dashed lines to the axes.
6. **Shade and label areas** (tax revenue, welfare loss, abnormal profit).
7. **Explain the diagram in your text**, referring to its labels.
8. Use the **context's names and numbers** where possible.

:::tip
A diagram earns marks only when it is relevant, accurate and explained. One correct, well-explained diagram beats three sketchy ones.
:::

#### Must-know diagrams
Open the [[diagrams|diagram library]] to drill all of them. The most frequently examined: demand and supply shifts; PED and revenue; specific tax with incidence; subsidy; price ceiling and floor; the four externality diagrams and their corrections; tradable permits; AD–AS (new classical and Keynesian) with gaps; demand-pull and cost-push inflation; money market; Lorenz curve; exchange rate appreciation/depreciation; tariff and quota; J-curve (HL); monopoly vs perfect competition (HL); PPC.
