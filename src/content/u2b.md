## 2.8 | Market failure: externalities and common pool resources | SL | Why free markets over- or under-produce, the four externality diagrams, policies to fix them, and the tragedy of the commons.

### market-failure | Market failure and the socially optimal output
@terms
Marginal private benefit (MPB) :: The extra benefit to the consumer of consuming one more unit; the market demand curve.
Marginal private cost (MPC) :: The extra cost to the producer of producing one more unit; the market supply curve.
Marginal social benefit (MSB) :: The extra benefit to society of one more unit: MPB plus any marginal external benefit.
Marginal social cost (MSC) :: The extra cost to society of one more unit: MPC plus any marginal external cost.
Externality :: A cost or benefit of production or consumption that falls on third parties not involved in the transaction, and is not reflected in the market price.
Socially optimal output :: The output where MSB = MSC, at which social welfare is maximised (allocative efficiency from society's view).
Overallocation :: Producing more than the socially optimal output (MSC > MSB for the extra units).
Underallocation :: Producing less than the socially optimal output (MSB > MSC for the missing units).
@end
Free markets reach **MPB = MPC**. Society is best off at **MSB = MSC**. When the two differ, the market **fails**: it allocates too many or too few resources to the good, and a **welfare loss** results.

Types of market failure in the course:
1. **Externalities** (negative and positive) and **common pool resources** (this topic)
2. **Public goods** ([[t2-9|2.9]])
3. **Asymmetric information** ([[t2-10|2.10]], HL)
4. **Market power** ([[t2-11|2.11]], HL)
5. **Inequity** ([[t2-12|2.12]])

:::tip
Market failure is about the **quantity** produced relative to the social optimum, not about prices being "too high". Explain using MSB, MSC and the welfare loss triangle.
:::

### neg-externalities | Negative externalities and demerit goods
@terms
Negative externality of production :: An external cost created when producing a good, so MSC > MPC (e.g. factory pollution).
Negative externality of consumption :: An external cost created when consuming a good, so MSB < MPB (e.g. passive smoking).
Demerit good :: A good that is harmful to consumers and society, overconsumed in a free market because of negative externalities and imperfect information (e.g. cigarettes, alcohol, sugary drinks).
@end
{{dia:ext-neg-prod}}
**Examples**: CO₂ from coal power stations (climate change), chemical run-off from farms polluting rivers, noise from airports, deforestation for palm oil. The market produces Qm > Qopt and the price is too low because it does not include the external cost.
{{dia:ext-neg-cons}}
**Examples**: smoking (passive smoking, healthcare costs), alcohol (accidents, violence), traffic congestion, single-use plastics littering, antibiotic overuse (resistance).

#### Demerit goods
Overconsumed because of **negative consumption externalities** and because consumers underestimate the harm to themselves (imperfect information, addiction, bounded self-control). Governments respond with taxes, bans, age limits, advertising restrictions and education.

:::hl Calculating the welfare loss
The welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from Qopt to Qm: **½ × base × height**, where the base is the vertical gap between the social curve and the private curve at Qm, and the height is (Qm − Qopt).
Example: at Qm = 800 the gap between MSC and MSB is $30; Qopt = 600. Welfare loss = ½ × 30 × (800 − 600) = **$3,000**.
:::

### pos-externalities | Positive externalities and merit goods
@terms
Positive externality of production :: An external benefit created when producing a good, so MSC < MPC (e.g. firms training workers, R&D spillovers).
Positive externality of consumption :: An external benefit created when consuming a good, so MSB > MPB (e.g. vaccinations).
Merit good :: A good that benefits the consumer and society, underconsumed in a free market because of positive externalities and imperfect information (e.g. education, healthcare, vaccines).
@end
{{dia:ext-pos-prod}}
**Examples**: a firm's training of workers who later move to other firms; R&D that other firms can build on; a beekeeper's bees pollinating nearby orchards; a company restoring a historic building that improves the area.
{{dia:ext-pos-cons}}
**Examples**: vaccinations (herd immunity), education (a more productive workforce, lower crime, better-informed voters), healthcare, using public transport instead of cars, installing solar panels.

#### Merit goods
Underconsumed because the market ignores external benefits, consumers underestimate private benefits (they may not see the long-term returns to education), and low-income households cannot afford them. Responses: subsidies, direct provision (free state schools and healthcare), legislation (compulsory schooling, vaccination requirements), information campaigns and nudges.

:::tip
Two supply curves → the externality is on the **production** side. Two demand curves → it is on the **consumption** side. Draw the right pair and label MSC/MPC or MSB/MPB exactly.
:::

### externality-policy | Policies to correct externalities
@terms
Pigouvian tax :: A tax set equal to the marginal external cost, to internalise a negative externality and bring output to the social optimum.
Carbon tax :: A tax on the carbon content of fossil fuels, to make polluters pay for the external cost of CO₂ emissions.
Internalising an externality :: Making the producer or consumer pay for (or receive) the external cost (or benefit), so that it enters their decision.
@end
#### Market-based policies
{{dia:pigou-tax}}
**Pigouvian and carbon taxes** shift MPC up to MSC (or MPB down to MSB for consumption). Examples: Sweden's carbon tax (around US$120 per tonne, among the world's highest, since 1991) alongside continued GDP growth; the UK sugar levy on soft drinks (2018) which pushed firms to cut sugar content; congestion charges in London, Stockholm, Singapore and New York (2025).
{{dia:subsidy-merit}}
**Subsidies** for merit goods and positive externalities: free or subsidised vaccines, EV purchase grants, solar panel feed-in tariffs, R&D tax credits.

:::eval Pigouvian / carbon taxes | Problems
+ "Polluter pays": fair and internalises the externality.
+ Market-based: firms choose the cheapest way to cut pollution, giving a continuing incentive to innovate.
+ Raise revenue that can fund green investment or offset regressive effects.
- Hard to measure the external cost in money, so the tax may be set wrongly.
- Inelastic demand means output may fall little.
- Regressive: hurts low-income households most.
- Firms may relocate to countries without the tax (carbon leakage); political resistance.
:::
:::eval Subsidies | Problems
+ Increase consumption of merit goods towards the optimum.
+ Popular and help low-income groups.
- Opportunity cost for government budgets.
- Difficult to calculate the correct amount; risk of firms keeping the subsidy.
- Can create dependency; hard to remove later.
:::

#### Command-and-control and other policies
| Policy | Example | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| **Legislation and regulation** | Bans on smoking in public places; emissions limits; bans on single-use plastics (EU 2021); ULEZ zones | Direct, certain effect; easy to understand | Monitoring and enforcement costs; no incentive to go beyond the standard; black markets |
| **Education and awareness** | Anti-smoking campaigns; health warnings; campaigns on the value of vaccination | Changes preferences in the long run, shifting MPB | Slow; costly; effect uncertain |
| **Nudges** | Opt-out organ donation; calorie labels | Cheap; preserve choice | Small effects ([[e-behavioural|2.4]]) |
| **Direct provision** | State-funded schools and hospitals; vaccination programmes | Guarantees access | High cost; possible inefficiency |

:::tip
For a 15-mark evaluation, use this frame for each policy: **effectiveness** (how far towards Qopt?), **measurement problems**, **costs and opportunity cost**, **stakeholders** (who gains, who loses: equity), **short run vs long run**, and **government failure** risk. Finish with a context-dependent judgement.
:::

### permits | Tradable permits and international agreements
@terms
Tradable permits :: Permits issued by a government allowing firms to emit a set quantity of pollution; firms can buy and sell them, so the total pollution is capped (cap and trade).
International agreement :: A treaty between countries to tackle a shared problem, such as the Paris Agreement on climate change.
@end
{{dia:permits}}
How cap and trade works: the authority sets a **cap**; permits are allocated free or auctioned; firms that cut emissions cheaply sell spare permits; others buy them. The market finds the cheapest way to meet the cap. The cap is cut over time.

:::eval Tradable permits | Problems
+ Total pollution is certain (the cap), unlike a tax.
+ Pollution is cut where it is cheapest; incentive to innovate.
+ Auctions raise revenue.
- Setting the cap is hard: the EU ETS issued too many permits after 2008 and prices collapsed below €10/tonne; after reforms they rose above €80.
- Free allocations to powerful lobbies; windfall profits.
- Monitoring and administrative costs; carbon leakage.
- Price volatility makes investment planning hard.
:::

**Carbon tax vs permits**: a tax gives certainty over the **price** of pollution; permits give certainty over the **quantity**.

#### International agreements
Climate change is a **global** negative externality, so national policies alone are weak (free-riding by other countries). Examples: the **Montreal Protocol** (1987) phased out ozone-destroying CFCs, widely seen as a success; the **Kyoto Protocol** (1997); the **Paris Agreement** (2015), with nationally determined contributions to keep warming well below 2°C. Weaknesses: no enforcement, countries can withdraw (the USA left Paris twice), conflicts between developed and developing countries over responsibility and finance.

### common-pool | Common pool resources and the tragedy of the commons
@terms
Common pool resource :: A natural resource that is rivalrous (one person's use reduces what is left for others) but non-excludable (people cannot easily be prevented from using it), e.g. fish stocks, forests, groundwater.
Tragedy of the commons :: The overuse and depletion of a common pool resource, because each user acts in their own interest without considering the cost to others.
Rivalrous :: When one person's use of a good reduces its availability to others.
Non-excludable :: When it is impossible or very costly to prevent people from using a good.
Collective self-governance :: Communities managing common resources themselves through shared rules and monitoring, as studied by Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize 2009).
Unsustainable resource use :: Using resources faster than they can regenerate, harming future generations.
@end
Because no one owns a common pool resource, each user gains the full benefit of using more while the cost (depletion) is shared by all. The result is **overuse**: overfishing (Atlantic cod collapse off Newfoundland, 1992), deforestation in the Amazon, depletion of groundwater in India and California, overgrazing, and the atmosphere as a dump for greenhouse gases.

These are threats to **sustainability**: today's use reduces what future generations will have.

#### Solutions
| Solution | Example | Evaluation |
| **Government regulation** | Fishing quotas (EU Common Fisheries Policy), protected areas, logging bans | Monitoring is costly; illegal activity; quotas can be set too high |
| **Tradable quotas / permits** | Iceland and New Zealand individual transferable fishing quotas | Effective in some fisheries; may concentrate ownership |
| **Legal property rights** | Granting indigenous communities titles to forest land (e.g. Amazon, Darién Gap) | Owners have an incentive to protect; enforcement needed |
| **Collective self-governance** | Swiss alpine pastures, Spanish huerta irrigation, community forests in Nepal | Ostrom showed it can work where communities are small and stable; harder for global resources |
| **International agreements** | UN High Seas Treaty (2023), Paris Agreement | Needed for global commons; weak enforcement |
| **Taxes / subsidies** | Carbon taxes; subsidies for clean technology | Market-based; measurement problems |

:::tip
Use common pool resources as the bridge to **sustainability** and **4.7**: the tragedy of the commons, fossil fuel use and climate change are all linked to SDGs 13, 14 and 15.
:::

## 2.9 | Market failure: public goods | SL | Non-rivalry, non-excludability, the free-rider problem and why governments provide public goods.

### public-goods | Public goods and the free-rider problem
@terms
Public good :: A good that is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, e.g. national defence, street lighting, lighthouses, flood defences.
Private good :: A good that is rivalrous and excludable, e.g. a sandwich.
Non-rivalrous :: When one person's consumption does not reduce the amount available to others.
Free-rider problem :: When people can benefit from a good without paying for it, so private firms cannot make a profit and will not supply it.
Quasi-public good :: A good with some but not all features of a public good, e.g. a toll-free road that can become congested.
@end
| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
| **Rivalrous** | Private goods (food, clothes) | Common pool resources (fish stocks) |
| **Non-rivalrous** | Club goods (streaming services, toll roads) | **Public goods** (defence, street lights) |

Because people cannot be excluded, they can **free ride**: enjoy the good without paying. Firms cannot charge, so the market provides **none** (or far too little): a **missing market**, the most extreme form of market failure.

#### Government responses
- **Direct provision** funded by taxation (defence, police, flood barriers like the Thames Barrier).
- **Contracting out**: government pays private firms to supply (street cleaning, road building).
- **Subsidies** to private suppliers.

:::eval Government provision | Problems
+ Provides goods the market would not supply at all.
+ Free at the point of use, so access for all, which improves equity.
- Opportunity cost: tax revenue has other uses.
- Government must estimate how much people value the good (hard to measure).
- Risk of inefficiency without competition; political priorities may distort spending.
:::

:::warn
Do not confuse **public goods** with **merit goods**. Firms do provide merit goods (private schools, private hospitals) because people can be charged and excluded; the problem is underconsumption. Firms provide no public goods because of the free-rider problem. Also: "provided by the government" does not make something a public good.
:::

## 2.10 | Market failure: asymmetric information | HL | When one side knows more than the other: adverse selection, moral hazard, and the responses.

### asym-info | Asymmetric information, adverse selection and moral hazard | HL
@terms
Asymmetric information :: When one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other, leading to market failure.
Adverse selection :: When asymmetric information before a transaction leads to the wrong people being selected into a market, e.g. high-risk people buying more insurance, or sellers of poor-quality used cars driving good cars out (the "market for lemons").
Moral hazard :: When one party takes more risks after a transaction because someone else bears the cost, e.g. insured drivers driving less carefully, or banks taking risks expecting a bailout.
Signalling :: When the better-informed party reveals information to the other, e.g. warranties, qualifications, brand reputation.
Screening :: When the less-informed party gathers information about the other, e.g. health checks before insurance, credit scores, interviews.
@end
#### Where it happens
- **Sellers know more**: used cars (Akerlof's "lemons"), second-hand goods, financial products, food quality, medical treatment, tradespeople's quality.
- **Buyers know more**: insurance (the customer knows their own health or driving habits), loans (the borrower knows their ability to repay).

#### Adverse selection vs moral hazard
| | Timing | Example | Result |
| **Adverse selection** | Before the deal | High-risk people buy health insurance; insurers raise premiums; low-risk people leave | Market shrinks or collapses |
| **Moral hazard** | After the deal | Banks "too big to fail" took huge risks before 2008; insured people take less care | Excessive risk, costs pushed onto others |

#### Responses
| Response | Examples | Limitations |
| **Legislation and regulation** | Consumer protection laws, mandatory disclosure, licensing of doctors, financial regulation after 2008 (Basel III), compulsory insurance | Costs of enforcement; regulatory capture |
| **Provision of information by government** | Food labelling, hygiene ratings, energy-efficiency labels, health campaigns | Consumers may ignore it; costly |
| **Signalling (private)** | Warranties, certified pre-owned cars, degrees, brand reputation, online reviews | Fake reviews; costly to signal |
| **Screening (private)** | Medical checks, credit checks, car inspections, insurance excess ("deductibles") | Costly; privacy concerns; may exclude vulnerable people |

:::tip
Asymmetric information is also a strong evaluation point elsewhere: it explains why merit goods are underconsumed and demerit goods overconsumed, and why financial crises happen.
:::

## 2.11 | Market failure: market power | HL | Costs and revenues, profit maximisation, the four market structures, efficiency, and how governments control market power.

### market-structures | Market structures and market power | HL
@terms
Market structure :: The characteristics of a market that determine how firms behave: number and size of firms, type of product, barriers to entry and information.
Market power :: The ability of a firm to influence the price of its product (price maker); also called monopoly power.
Barriers to entry :: Obstacles that make it difficult for new firms to enter a market, e.g. economies of scale, legal barriers, brand loyalty, control of resources, high start-up costs.
Price taker :: A firm that must accept the market price because it is too small to influence it.
Price maker :: A firm with enough market power to set its own price.
Concentration ratio :: The combined market share of the largest firms in an industry, e.g. CR4 = share of the four largest firms.
@end
| | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
| Number of firms | Very many | Many | A few dominant | One |
| Product | Homogeneous (identical) | Differentiated | Homogeneous or differentiated | Unique, no close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Price setting | Price taker | Some power | Interdependent price makers | Price maker |
| Long-run profit | Normal | Normal | Can be abnormal | Abnormal |
| Demand curve | Perfectly elastic | Downward, elastic | Downward (kinked?) | Downward, the market demand |
| Examples | Agricultural commodities, foreign exchange (approximations) | Restaurants, hairdressers, clothing | Supermarkets, airlines, smartphones, banks | Water utilities, patented drugs, Google in search |

#### Barriers to entry
- **Economies of scale** (natural monopoly): incumbents' low costs undercut entrants.
- **Legal barriers**: patents, copyrights, licences, franchises.
- **Control of essential resources** (De Beers and diamonds historically).
- **Brand loyalty and advertising** (Coca-Cola, Apple).
- **High sunk costs / start-up costs** (aircraft manufacturing: Airbus and Boeing).
- **Network effects** (social media, operating systems).
- **Anti-competitive behaviour**: predatory pricing, exclusive contracts.

### profit-max | Costs, revenues and profit maximisation | HL
@terms
Total cost (TC) :: All the costs of producing a given output: fixed costs plus variable costs.
Fixed costs :: Costs that do not change with output in the short run, e.g. rent, insurance.
Variable costs :: Costs that change with output, e.g. raw materials, hourly wages.
Average total cost (ATC) :: Total cost divided by output.
Average revenue (AR) :: Total revenue divided by quantity sold; equal to price.
Marginal revenue (MR) :: The additional revenue from selling one more unit.
Normal profit :: The minimum profit needed to keep a firm in its current industry; when TR = TC (economic costs include the opportunity cost of the entrepreneur).
Abnormal profit :: Profit above normal profit (TR > TC); also called supernormal or economic profit.
Loss :: When total revenue is less than total cost (TR < TC).
Profit maximisation rule :: A firm maximises profit by producing where MC = MR (with MC rising).
@end
{{dia:cost-curves}}
{{dia:revenue-curves}}
#### Economic profit
Economists include **opportunity costs** in costs, so **normal profit** (TR = TC) is the return just high enough to keep resources in their current use.
- **Abnormal profit**: TR > TC (AR > ATC). Attracts entry if barriers allow.
- **Normal profit**: TR = TC (AR = ATC).
- **Loss**: TR < TC. In the short run, produce if AR ≥ AVC; in the long run, leave if AR < ATC.

#### Why MC = MR?
If MR > MC, one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost: profit rises, so expand. If MC > MR, the last unit lost money: cut output. Profit is maximised where **MC = MR**.

| Output | MR ($) | MC ($) | Change in profit |
| 4th unit | 20 | 12 | +8 → produce |
| 5th unit | 18 | 18 | 0 → profit-maximising output |
| 6th unit | 16 | 24 | −8 → do not produce |

{{dia:lrac}}
:::calc Profit from a diagram or table
Instants PLC produces 3,000 units a month; price $60, ATC $64, AVC $56.
TR = 60 × 3,000 = $180,000. TC = 64 × 3,000 = $192,000. **Loss = $12,000**.
Should it keep producing in the short run? Yes: P ($60) > AVC ($56), so revenue covers variable costs and contributes $4 × 3,000 = $12,000 towards fixed costs of $24,000 (= (64 − 56) × 3,000). Shutting down would lose all $24,000.
:::

### perfect-competition | Perfect competition | HL
@terms
Perfect competition :: A market structure with very many small firms selling an identical product, perfect information, and no barriers to entry or exit.
Productive efficiency (firm) :: Producing at the lowest point of the average total cost curve.
Allocative efficiency (firm) :: Producing where price equals marginal cost (P = MC).
Shut-down price :: The price equal to minimum average variable cost, below which a firm stops producing in the short run.
Break-even price :: The price equal to minimum average total cost, at which a firm earns normal profit.
@end
**Assumptions**: very many buyers and sellers; homogeneous product; perfect information; perfect factor mobility; free entry and exit. Each firm is a **price taker**: its demand curve is perfectly elastic at the market price (P = AR = MR).
{{dia:pc-sr-profit}}
**Short run → long run (abnormal profit)**: profit attracts new firms; industry supply shifts right; the price falls until it reaches minimum ATC and only normal profit remains.
{{dia:pc-sr-loss}}
**Short run → long run (losses)**: firms leave; supply shifts left; price rises to minimum ATC.
{{dia:pc-lr}}
{{dia:pc-shutdown}}
#### Efficiency
In long-run equilibrium **P = MC** (allocatively efficient) and output is at **minimum ATC** (productively efficient). This is the benchmark.

:::eval Strengths | Weaknesses
+ Allocative and productive efficiency in the long run.
+ Low prices; no abnormal profit at consumers' expense; consumer sovereignty.
- Unrealistic assumptions: no real market fits fully (agriculture and forex come close).
- Firms are too small for economies of scale; no abnormal profit to fund R&D, so weak dynamic efficiency.
- Identical products mean no variety.
:::

### monopoly | Monopoly and natural monopoly | HL
@terms
Monopoly :: A market structure with a single seller of a product with no close substitutes, protected by high barriers to entry.
Natural monopoly :: A market where economies of scale are so large that one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms.
Dynamic efficiency :: Efficiency over time, through innovation, new products and falling costs.
@end
{{dia:monopoly}}
A monopolist faces the market demand curve, so AR slopes down and MR lies below it. It maximises profit at **MC = MR** and earns **abnormal profit in the long run** because barriers stop entry. (It can also make normal profit or short-run losses if demand is weak.)
{{dia:monopoly-welfare}}
#### Costs and benefits
| | Disadvantages | Possible advantages |
| Consumers | Higher prices, less output, less choice; loss of consumer surplus | Abnormal profit may fund innovation (pharmaceuticals); cross-subsidisation |
| Efficiency | Allocatively inefficient (P > MC); not productively efficient; X-inefficiency (lazy management) | Economies of scale may give lower costs than competitive firms |
| Workers and suppliers | Monopsony power over suppliers and workers; fewer employers | Higher wages from profits; large orders for suppliers |
| Economy | Welfare loss; inequality (profits to owners) | Global competitiveness of national champions; dynamic efficiency |

{{dia:natural-monopoly}}
**Natural monopoly** (water, rail networks, electricity grids): breaking it up would raise costs, so governments **regulate** prices (marginal-cost pricing = efficient but loss-making; average-cost pricing = break-even) or own the firm.

:::tip
Evaluate monopoly fairly: Amazon and Google became dominant partly by being very good at what they do (low costs, innovation). The question is whether they now abuse that power.
:::

### monopolistic-competition | Monopolistic competition | HL
@terms
Monopolistic competition :: A market structure with many firms selling differentiated products, with low barriers to entry.
Product differentiation :: Making a product different from rivals' through branding, quality, design, location or service.
Excess capacity :: When a firm produces below the output that minimises average total cost.
Non-price competition :: Competing through means other than price, such as advertising, branding, quality, loyalty schemes and after-sales service.
@end
{{dia:monopolistic-comp}}
Each firm has a little market power from **differentiation**, so its demand curve slopes down (but is fairly elastic: many substitutes). **Short run**: abnormal profit or loss. **Long run**: entry (or exit) shifts each firm's demand until AR is tangent to ATC: **normal profit**.

**Efficiency**: P > MC (allocatively inefficient) and output below minimum ATC (productively inefficient: **excess capacity**). But consumers gain **variety and choice**, which they value.

### oligopoly | Oligopoly, collusion and game theory | HL
@terms
Oligopoly :: A market dominated by a few large, interdependent firms with high barriers to entry.
Interdependence :: Each oligopolist's decisions affect its rivals, so each must consider how rivals will react.
Collusion :: An agreement between firms to limit competition, for example by fixing prices or sharing out markets.
Cartel :: A formal collusive agreement among firms or countries to restrict output and raise prices, e.g. OPEC.
Tacit collusion :: Cooperation without a formal agreement, e.g. price leadership, where firms follow the price set by a dominant firm.
Game theory :: The study of strategic decision-making when the outcome for each player depends on the choices of others.
Prisoner's dilemma :: A game in which both players would gain by cooperating, but each has an incentive to cheat, so both end up worse off.
Price war :: Repeated price cutting by rival firms to gain market share, reducing profits for all.
@end
#### Concentration ratios
A **CR4** of 74% means the four largest firms have 74% of sales. UK groceries (2022): Tesco 27%, Sainsbury's 15%, Asda 14%, Aldi 9% → CR4 ≈ 65%. A concentration ratio above about 50% for the top four suggests oligopoly.

#### Collusive vs non-collusive behaviour
Firms are tempted to **collude** to act like a monopoly and share abnormal profits. Collusion is easier with few firms, similar costs, high barriers, stable demand and weak regulation. **Formal collusion** is illegal in most countries (EU trucks cartel fined €2.9bn in 2016; US and EU fines on banks for rigging LIBOR and FX rates). **OPEC+** is a legal cartel of oil-producing countries. **Tacit collusion**: price leadership, following rivals' prices.

#### Game theory
| | **Firm B: high price** | **Firm B: low price** |
| **Firm A: high price** | A $10m, B $10m | A $2m, B $14m |
| **Firm A: low price** | A $14m, B $2m | A $5m, B $5m |
Whatever B does, A earns more by pricing low (14 > 10, 5 > 2), and the same for B. Both choose low and earn $5m: the **Nash equilibrium**. Both would be better off at high/high ($10m each) if they could trust each other: the **prisoner's dilemma**, which explains both price wars and the incentive to collude (and to cheat on collusion).

#### Price and non-price competition
Price competition is risky (price wars, e.g. budget airlines; UK supermarket price matching). Oligopolists prefer **non-price competition**: branding, advertising, loyalty cards, product innovation, sponsorship (Nike, Adidas), packaging, after-sales service, celebrity and influencer endorsement.
{{dia:kinked-demand}}
:::key Kinked demand curve
The kinked demand curve is a classic way to explain why oligopoly prices are "sticky"; it is a useful extension in essays on oligopoly even though the current guide focuses on game theory.
:::

### market-power-policy | Market power and government intervention | HL
@terms
Competition policy :: Government laws and regulators that promote competition and prevent abuse of market power, e.g. blocking mergers and fining cartels.
Nationalisation :: When the government takes ownership of a private firm or industry.
Price regulation :: Government control of the prices charged by firms with market power, e.g. price caps on utilities.
Monopsony :: A market with a single (or dominant) buyer, which can push down the prices it pays to suppliers or the wages it pays workers.
@end
#### Is market power good or bad?
:::eval Possible benefits | Costs
+ Economies of scale → lower costs and prices.
+ Abnormal profits fund R&D and innovation (dynamic efficiency).
+ Size helps firms compete globally.
- Higher prices, lower output, welfare loss.
- Less choice; less incentive to innovate or control costs (X-inefficiency).
- Abuse of dominance: predatory pricing, exclusive deals, monopsony squeezes on suppliers and workers.
- Political power and lobbying.
:::

#### Government responses
| Tool | How it works | Examples |
| **Competition law and regulators** | Ban cartels and abuse of dominance; investigate and block mergers | EU fined Google €2.4bn (2017, shopping) and €4.3bn (2018, Android); UK CMA blocked then cleared revised Microsoft–Activision deal (2023); US v Google search ruling (2024) |
| **Fines** | Penalties large enough to deter | EU Digital Markets Act (2024): fines up to 10% of global turnover |
| **Price regulation** | Caps on prices of natural monopolies | UK water (Ofwat) and energy price cap (Ofgem) |
| **Breaking up monopolies** | Split dominant firms | Standard Oil (1911), AT&T (1984) |
| **Nationalisation** | Government ownership | UK rail franchises returned to public ownership from 2025 |
| **Protecting suppliers and workers** | Laws against monopsony abuse, minimum wages | UK Groceries Code Adjudicator |

:::tip
Government failure applies here too: regulators may lack information, be slow, or suffer **regulatory capture** (being influenced by the firms they regulate).
:::

## 2.12 | The market's inability to achieve equity | SL | Why free markets can produce unequal outcomes, and how this links to Unit 3's inequality policies.

### equity | Markets and inequality
@terms
Equality :: Everyone having the same income, wealth or opportunities; a positive (measurable) idea.
Inequity :: Unfairness in the distribution of income, wealth or opportunity; a normative judgement.
Factor endowments :: The quantity and quality of factors of production a person or country owns.
@end
Free markets reward people according to the **factors of production they own** and how scarce those factors are. People with valuable skills, capital or land earn high incomes; those with little to sell earn little. There is no mechanism in the market that ensures a fair outcome.

#### Why markets create inequality
- **Unequal ownership of factors**: wealth and capital are concentrated; returns to capital can grow faster than wages (Piketty's r > g argument).
- **Differences in human capital**: education and skills command high wages.
- **Inheritance**: wealth passes between generations.
- **Market power**: monopolists and powerful employers capture income.
- **Discrimination**: by gender, ethnicity, caste or disability.
- **Technological change**: rewards high-skilled workers and replaces routine jobs.
- **Unequal access to merit goods**: low-income families cannot afford good education or healthcare, so inequality persists across generations.

#### The circular flow view
Households receive factor incomes in proportion to what they own; government can redistribute by **taxing** (leakage) higher incomes and making **transfer payments** and providing **public services** (injection), which reduces inequality ([[t3-4|3.4]]).

:::tip
Equity is a normative concept: what counts as "fair" is a value judgement. In essays, say whose idea of fairness you are using (equality of outcome vs equality of opportunity).
:::
