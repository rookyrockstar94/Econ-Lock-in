## 1.1 | What is economics? | SL | Economics as a social science, scarcity and choice, the PPC and the circular flow of income.

### social-science | Economics as a social science
@terms
Economics :: The study of how individuals, firms and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited needs and wants.
Social science :: A discipline that studies human behaviour in society; because people are unpredictable, its findings are less certain than those of the natural sciences.
Microeconomics :: The study of individual decision-makers (consumers, firms, workers) and of individual markets.
Macroeconomics :: The study of the economy as a whole: total output, the average price level, unemployment, growth and the role of government policy.
Economic model :: A simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, used to explain and predict economic behaviour.
@end
Economics is a **social science**: it studies people and how they interact, which makes it messier than physics or chemistry. People change their minds, react to news and copy each other, and it is impossible to run controlled experiments on a whole country. Economists therefore rely on **models**, simplified pictures of reality that keep a few variables and hold everything else constant.

#### Micro vs macro
| Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
| One market, e.g. coffee or rental housing | The whole economy, e.g. Singapore |
| The price of a single good | The average price level (inflation, deflation) |
| Demand by consumers of one good | Aggregate demand: total spending in the economy |
| Supply by firms in an industry | Aggregate supply: total output |
| A tax on cigarettes | Income tax rates, interest rates, the budget |
| Why wages differ between jobs | Unemployment across the economy |

#### Thinking like an economist
- Choose which variables matter, and which can be ignored for now.
- Distinguish **correlation** from **causation**: ice-cream sales and car thefts both rise in summer, but one does not cause the other.
- Two economists can study the same data and reach different conclusions because they focus on different variables or hold different value judgements. That is a normal feature of a social science, not a failure.

:::tip
In any Paper 1 (b) or Paper 2 (g) answer, questioning a model's **assumptions** (rational consumers, perfect information, ceteris paribus) is one of the quickest routes into evaluation.
:::

### scarcity | Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost
@terms
Scarcity :: The basic economic problem: resources are finite but human needs and wants are unlimited, so choices must be made.
Factors of production :: The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship.
Land :: All natural resources used in production, including minerals, forests, water and the land itself; its reward is rent.
Labour :: The physical and mental effort of people in production; its reward is wages.
Capital :: Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services, such as machines, tools, buildings and infrastructure; its reward is interest.
Entrepreneurship :: The organising and risk-taking factor that combines the other factors to produce goods and services; its reward is profit.
Opportunity cost :: The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
Free good :: A good that is not scarce, so it has no opportunity cost (e.g. air, sunlight).
Economic good :: A good that is scarce, so producing or consuming it has an opportunity cost.
Needs :: Things essential for survival, such as food, water, shelter and clothing.
Wants :: Things people desire beyond their basic needs; they are unlimited.
@end
**Scarcity** is why economics exists. Because resources (the **factors of production**) are limited while wants are not, every decision to use resources one way means not using them another way. That sacrifice is the **opportunity cost**.

| Factor | What it is | Factor income |
| Land | Natural resources: oil, timber, fish stocks, farmland | Rent |
| Labour | Human effort, skilled and unskilled | Wages |
| Capital | Man-made aids to production: machines, factories, roads, computers | Interest |
| Entrepreneurship | Organising the other factors and bearing risk | Profit |

**Human capital** is the skills, knowledge and health of workers; investment in education and health raises it. **Physical capital** is machinery and infrastructure.

#### Opportunity cost in practice
- A student who spends Saturday working a $90 shift gives up revising for the economics test. If revising was the next best option, the opportunity cost is the lost revision, not the $90.
- A government that spends $2bn on a new airport cannot spend that $2bn on hospitals.
- Opportunity cost is not just money: it includes time, effort and alternative uses of resources.

#### Free goods and economic goods
Free goods have zero opportunity cost (air, sunlight, seawater). Most goods are economic goods. Goods can change category: clean drinking water was once free in many places but has become an economic good as populations grew and rivers were polluted.

:::warn
- Writing "opportunity cost is the cost of the product". It is the **next best alternative forgone**, one option only, not all options.
- Treating money as a factor of production. Money is a means of buying factors; capital means physical capital.
:::

### econ-questions | The basic economic questions and economic systems
@terms
Free market economy :: An economy in which resources are privately owned and allocated by the price mechanism, with minimal government intervention.
Planned economy :: An economy in which the government owns most resources and decides what, how and for whom to produce (also called a command economy).
Mixed economy :: An economy in which both the market and the government allocate resources; all modern economies are mixed.
Price mechanism :: The system in which changes in prices, determined by demand and supply, signal and ration resources and give incentives to buyers and sellers.
@end
Every society must answer three questions:
1. **What to produce?** Which goods and services, and in what quantities?
2. **How to produce?** Which methods: labour-intensive or capital-intensive? Which technology?
3. **For whom to produce?** How is output distributed among people?

| System | What? | How? | For whom? | Examples |
| Free market | Consumer demand via prices | The most profitable (least cost) method | Those willing and able to pay | Hong Kong comes closest |
| Planned (command) | Government plan | Government directives, often to maximise jobs | Government decides; often more equal | Former USSR, North Korea, Cuba |
| Mixed | Markets plus government | Mainly firms, with regulation | Market income plus welfare state, public services | Almost every country today: USA, UK, France, China, India |

:::eval Free markets | Planned systems
+ Price signals allocate resources efficiently and quickly.
+ Profit motive drives innovation and choice.
+ No large bureaucracy needed to coordinate.
- Planned: shortages and surpluses because planners lack information.
- Planned: weak incentives to work hard or innovate.
- Planned: can guarantee basic needs and reduce inequality.
Free markets also fail: they ignore externalities, underprovide public and merit goods, and can create large inequality. That is the case for a **mixed economy**, and the subject of most of [[t2-8|Unit 2's market failure topics]].
:::

### ppc | The production possibilities curve (PPC)
@terms
Production possibilities curve (PPC) :: A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, with given technology.
Productive efficiency :: Producing at the lowest possible cost, i.e. on the PPC (in micro: at the minimum of average total cost).
Allocative efficiency :: Producing the combination of goods society values most, where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost.
Actual growth :: An increase in real output, shown by a movement from inside the PPC towards it.
Potential growth :: An increase in an economy's productive capacity, shown by an outward shift of the PPC.
Capital goods :: Goods used to produce other goods, such as machinery and buildings.
Consumer goods :: Goods bought by households for final consumption.
@end
The PPC is the first model in the course and illustrates **scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, unemployment and growth** all at once.
{{dia:ppc}}
#### What the PPC shows
- **Scarcity**: points outside the curve are unattainable with current resources and technology.
- **Choice**: society must pick one point on or inside the curve.
- **Opportunity cost**: moving along the curve means giving up some of one good to get more of the other.
- **Efficiency**: points on the curve are productively efficient; points inside involve **unemployment or inefficient use of resources**.

#### Shape: increasing vs constant opportunity cost
A **concave (bowed-out)** PPC shows increasing opportunity cost: resources are not equally good at producing both goods, so as more of one is made, ever more of the other must be given up. A **straight-line** PPC shows constant opportunity cost, where resources are perfectly transferable (e.g. a farm switching between two similar crops).

#### Shifts of the PPC
{{dia:ppc-growth}}
- **Outward shift (potential growth)**: more or better factors of production (immigration, investment in capital, education and training, discovery of resources) or better technology.
- **Inward shift**: natural disasters (Japan's 2011 earthquake and tsunami), war (Ukraine since 2022), large-scale emigration, depletion of resources.
- **Biased shift**: technology improves in one industry only, so the curve pivots out on that good's axis.

#### Capital goods vs consumer goods
{{dia:ppc-cap-cons}}
An economy choosing more capital goods today (and fewer consumer goods) will have a larger PPC tomorrow. This is a key idea for growth and development: poor countries struggle to sacrifice current consumption to invest.

:::tip
Label PPC axes with two named goods (or capital and consumer goods), **never** price and quantity. In Paper 1 (a) questions on scarcity or opportunity cost, the PPC is often the expected diagram.
:::

### circular-flow-intro | The circular flow of income model
@terms
Circular flow of income :: A model showing how money flows between households and firms (and government, financial sector and the rest of the world) in exchange for factors and goods.
Injections :: Additions to the circular flow from outside the household-firm loop: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X).
Leakages :: Withdrawals from the circular flow: saving (S), taxes (T) and import spending (M).
@end
The simplest model has two sectors: **households** sell factors of production to **firms** and receive incomes; households spend those incomes on firms' goods and services. Money flows one way, real goods and factors the other.
{{dia:circular-flow}}
- **Leakages (withdrawals)**: saving (S), taxes (T), imports (M) take money out of the flow.
- **Injections**: investment (I), government spending (G), exports (X) add money.
- If **injections > leakages** the economy expands; if **leakages > injections** it contracts. Equilibrium: I + G + X = S + T + M.
- The model shows **interdependence**: households, firms, the government, banks and other countries all depend on each other's spending.

The full analysis, including the three ways of measuring national income, is in [[e-measuring-output|3.1 Measuring economic activity]].

### key-concepts | The nine key concepts
@terms
Sustainability :: Meeting the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
Equity :: Fairness in the distribution of income, wealth or opportunities; a normative concept, distinct from equality.
Interdependence :: The way economic agents and countries rely on each other through trade, finance and markets.
Economic well-being :: A multidimensional idea of prosperity and quality of life: financial security, meeting basic needs, the ability to make choices and to maintain an adequate income over time.
Intervention :: Government involvement in the workings of markets.
@end
The IB guide is built around nine concepts. Every Internal Assessment commentary must use a **different** key concept, and strong Paper 1 and 2 answers use them to frame evaluation.

| Concept | What it means | Where it bites |
| Scarcity | Limited resources vs unlimited wants | PPC, opportunity cost, common pool resources |
| Choice | Scarcity forces choices with opportunity costs | Consumer choice, government budgets |
| Efficiency | Getting the most useful output from inputs; allocative and productive efficiency | Market structures, market failure, trade |
| Equity | Fairness; differs from equality | Taxes, price controls, poverty, development |
| Economic well-being | Living standards and quality of life, now and in future | GDP vs well-being, HDI, development |
| Sustainability | Meeting today's needs without harming future generations | Externalities, SDGs, climate policy |
| Change | Economies are always changing; economics studies change | Shifts in curves, growth, structural change |
| Interdependence | Agents and countries rely on one another | Circular flow, trade, exchange rates, globalisation |
| Intervention | Government involvement in markets | Taxes, subsidies, regulation, policy debates |

:::tip
When you mention a concept in an essay, use it: "the tax raises **efficiency** by internalising the externality, but harms **equity** because it is regressive" is evaluation; just naming "equity" is not.
:::

## 1.2 | How do economists approach the world? | SL | The social scientific method, positive vs normative economics, and how economic thinking has changed since Adam Smith.

### methodology | Positive vs normative economics and the scientific method
@terms
Positive economics :: Statements about what is, was or will be, which can be tested against evidence.
Normative economics :: Statements based on value judgements about what ought to be, which cannot be tested.
Ceteris paribus :: "All other things being equal": holding all other variables constant to isolate the effect of one change.
Hypothesis :: A testable statement about the relationship between variables.
Empirical evidence :: Information gathered by observation and measurement, used to test hypotheses.
Refutation :: Showing that a hypothesis is false by testing it against evidence; theories can be refuted but never finally proven.
Value judgement :: A view based on opinions or beliefs about what is good or fair.
@end
#### Positive vs normative
| Positive | Normative |
| Can be tested with evidence | Based on value judgements |
| "UK unemployment rose to 4.8% in 2025." | "The government should cut unemployment before it worries about inflation." |
| "A higher tobacco tax reduces smoking among teenagers." | "Smoking should be banned in all public places." |
| "Raising the minimum wage reduced wage inequality." | "The minimum wage is too low to live on and is unfair." |

A positive statement can be **wrong**; it is still positive if it can be tested. Normative statements often contain "should", "ought", "fair", "too much".

#### The social scientific method
1. Observe a pattern and form a **hypothesis** (e.g. "higher interest rates reduce consumer spending").
2. Gather **empirical** data: statistics, surveys, natural experiments.
3. Test the hypothesis, holding other variables constant (**ceteris paribus**) as far as possible.
4. Accept it provisionally or **refute** it. A theory that survives repeated testing becomes part of an economic model.

Economists cannot run controlled laboratory experiments on economies, so they use **ceteris paribus** in models, statistical techniques on real data, and increasingly randomised controlled trials (e.g. Duflo and Banerjee's development research, Nobel Prize 2019) and behavioural experiments.

#### Rational economic decision-making
Standard models assume agents are **rational**: consumers maximise **utility**, firms maximise **profit**, workers balance pay and conditions, and governments maximise social welfare. Behavioural economics challenges these assumptions (see [[e-behavioural|2.4]]).

:::tip
In a 2-mark "distinguish" question, give one example of each and say why: "positive: can be tested with data; normative: contains a value judgement."
:::

### thought | The history of economic thought
@terms
Mercantilism :: The 16th–18th century view that national wealth came from accumulating gold through exports and restricting imports.
Laissez-faire :: The idea that governments should leave markets alone, with minimal intervention.
Invisible hand :: Adam Smith's idea that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets unintentionally promote the good of society.
Say's law :: "Supply creates its own demand": production generates the income needed to buy that output.
Marginalism :: Analysing decisions at the margin, i.e. the extra benefit and extra cost of one more unit.
Keynesian economics :: The view that markets can stay in recession for long periods and that governments should manage aggregate demand, especially through fiscal policy.
Monetarism :: The view, led by Milton Friedman, that inflation is caused by excessive growth of the money supply and that governments should keep monetary growth steady.
Behavioural economics :: A branch of economics that uses insights from psychology to explain why people do not always act rationally.
Circular economy :: An economic system that eliminates waste, keeps products and materials in use and regenerates natural systems.
Doughnut economics :: Kate Raworth's model in which economies should meet everyone's basic needs (the social foundation) without exceeding the planet's ecological limits (the ecological ceiling).
@end
| Period | Thinkers and ideas | Context |
| 1500s–1700s | **Mercantilism**: wealth = gold; run trade surpluses, protect industry | Rise of European nation states and colonial empires |
| 1776 | **Adam Smith**, *The Wealth of Nations*: division of labour, the invisible hand, laissez-faire, free trade | Start of the Industrial Revolution; reaction against mercantilism |
| 1817 | **David Ricardo**: comparative advantage | Debate over the Corn Laws |
| 1803 | **Jean-Baptiste Say**: Say's law, "supply creates its own demand" | Classical macroeconomics: markets self-correct |
| 1867 | **Karl Marx**, *Das Kapital*: capitalism exploits labour, creates inequality, will collapse into revolution | Harsh factory conditions; growing inequality |
| 1870s | **Marginal revolution** (Jevons, Menger, Walras; later Marshall): value comes from utility at the margin; demand and supply diagrams | Mathematical economics |
| 1936 | **John Maynard Keynes**, *The General Theory*: demand-deficient unemployment can persist; government should spend in a slump | Great Depression; Say's law seemed to fail |
| 1970s–80s | **Milton Friedman** and the monetarist/new classical counter-revolution: control money supply, free markets, supply-side policies, privatisation | Stagflation after oil shocks; Thatcher and Reagan governments |
| 2000s– | **Behavioural economics** (Kahneman, Thaler), renewed Keynesian policy after the 2008 crisis, **Doughnut economics** (Raworth), the **circular economy** | Global financial crisis, Covid-19, climate change, inequality |

#### Why it matters for exams
Many evaluation points come down to this history: **Keynesians vs new classical economists** on whether the economy self-corrects (AD–AS, [[t3-2|3.2]]), the shape of AS, the effectiveness of fiscal vs monetary policy, and the role of government. Knowing who argued what lets you present balanced perspectives.

:::ex Doughnut economics in Amsterdam
In 2020 Amsterdam became the first city to adopt the Doughnut model as the basis for policy, using it to guide housing, circular-economy building rules and reductions in material use. It is a real example of an alternative to targeting GDP growth alone.
:::
