# U3 | Macroeconomics | The economy as a whole: measuring output, AD–AS, growth, unemployment, inflation, inequality, and the policies governments use.

## 3.1 | Measuring economic activity and illustrating its variations | SL | GDP and GNI, real vs nominal, per capita and PPP, GDP as a measure of well-being, and the business cycle.

### measuring-output | The circular flow and three ways to measure national income
@terms
Gross domestic product (GDP) :: The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period, usually a year.
National income :: The total income earned in an economy; output = income = expenditure in the circular flow.
Output approach :: Measuring GDP by adding the value of output (value added) produced by every industry.
Income approach :: Measuring GDP by adding all incomes earned: wages, rent, interest and profit.
Expenditure approach :: Measuring GDP by adding all spending on final goods and services: C + I + G + (X − M).
Consumption (C) :: Spending by households on goods and services.
Investment (I) :: Spending by firms on capital goods, such as machinery, buildings and stocks.
Government spending (G) :: Spending by the government on goods and services (not transfer payments).
Net exports (X − M) :: The value of exports minus the value of imports.
@end
{{dia:circular-flow}}
Because every dollar spent is a dollar of income for someone and corresponds to a dollar of output, **national output = national income = national expenditure**. Three methods should give the same answer (differences arise from measurement errors).

| Approach | Adds up | Watch out for |
| **Output** | Value added by each industry (agriculture, manufacturing, services) | Count only value added to avoid **double counting** of intermediate goods |
| **Income** | Wages + rent + interest + profit | Exclude transfer payments (pensions, benefits): no output is produced |
| **Expenditure** | C + I + G + (X − M) | Exclude second-hand sales and financial transactions |

:::calc Expenditure method
Vietnam (illustrative, US$bn): C = 180, I = 70, G = 17, X = 280, M = 260.
GDP = 180 + 70 + 17 + (280 − 260) = **$287bn**.
:::

:::tip
Injections > leakages → national income rises. Always consider the **size** of each change: a big rise in G can outweigh higher savings and imports together.
:::

### ni-terms | GDP, GNI, real vs nominal, per capita and PPP
@terms
Gross national income (GNI) :: GDP plus net income from abroad (income earned by nationals abroad minus income earned by foreigners in the country).
Nominal GDP :: GDP measured at current prices, not adjusted for inflation.
Real GDP :: GDP adjusted for inflation, measured at constant (base-year) prices.
GDP deflator :: A price index used to convert nominal GDP into real GDP.
GDP per capita :: GDP divided by the population; a measure of average output or income per person.
Purchasing power parity (PPP) :: An exchange rate that equalises the purchasing power of different currencies, so that the same basket of goods costs the same in each country.
@end
#### GDP vs GNI
**GNI = GDP + net income from abroad** (profits, interest and wages flowing in minus those flowing out). Countries with large foreign-owned multinationals have **GNI < GDP** (Ireland: GNI is about 25% below GDP; many resource-exporting developing countries where foreign mining firms repatriate profits). Countries receiving large remittances or overseas investment income may have **GNI > GDP** (e.g. Bangladesh, Philippines, Japan).

#### Nominal vs real
Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rise. **Real GDP** removes inflation, so it shows the change in actual output.

:::formula Converting nominal to real
Real GDP = \frac{nominal GDP}{GDP deflator} × 100
GNI = GDP + net income from abroad
GDP per capita = \frac{GDP}{population}
:::
:::calc Real GDP
Nominal GDP in 2021 = $840bn; GDP deflator = 105 (base year 2020 = 100). Real GDP = 840 ÷ 105 × 100 = **$800bn**. If real GDP in 2020 was $780bn, real growth = (800 − 780) ÷ 780 × 100 = **2.56%**.
:::

#### Per capita and PPP
Comparing **totals** is misleading for countries with very different populations (India's GDP is larger than the UK's but its GDP per capita is about 1/20th). **Per capita** adjusts for population.
Market exchange rates do not reflect what money buys locally. **PPP** adjusts for differences in the cost of living, so GDP per capita at PPP is the best measure for comparing living standards across countries (India's GDP per capita is about $2,700 at market rates but around $10,000 at PPP).

:::tip
"At constant prices" means **real**; "at current prices" means **nominal**. For international comparisons of living standards, use **real GDP/GNI per capita at PPP**.
:::

### gdp-wellbeing | GDP as a measure of well-being and alternative measures
@terms
Informal economy :: Economic activity that is not recorded or taxed, e.g. cash-in-hand work and street vending.
OECD Better Life Index :: An index comparing well-being across countries using 11 dimensions, such as housing, income, jobs, health, environment and life satisfaction.
Happy Planet Index :: An index that measures how efficiently countries turn ecological resources into long, happy lives (well-being × life expectancy ÷ ecological footprint).
World Happiness Report :: A UN-backed annual survey ranking countries by how people rate their lives.
@end
#### Limitations of GDP/GNI as a measure of well-being
| Limitation | Explanation |
| **Non-market output** | Unpaid work (childcare, housework, volunteering) is excluded |
| **Informal economy** | Unrecorded activity, very large in developing countries (often 30–60% of activity) |
| **Quality of life** | Leisure, working hours, health, safety and freedom are excluded |
| **Composition of output** | Spending on defence, prisons or pollution clean-up raises GDP but not well-being |
| **Distribution** | GDP per capita is an average; it hides inequality |
| **Environment** | Depletion of resources and pollution are not subtracted |
| **Quality improvements** | Better products at the same price are under-counted |
| **Data accuracy** | Measurement errors; revisions; differences in methods between countries |

#### Alternative measures
- **OECD Better Life Index**: 11 dimensions (housing, income, jobs, community, education, environment, civic engagement, health, life satisfaction, safety, work–life balance); users can weight them.
- **Happy Planet Index**: well-being and life expectancy relative to ecological footprint; Costa Rica and Vanuatu score high; rich high-consumption countries score low.
- **World Happiness Report**: life-evaluation surveys (Finland has ranked first every year since 2018).
- **Human Development Index (HDI)** and other composite indicators are covered in [[t4-8|4.8]].

### business-cycle | The business cycle
@terms
Business cycle :: The fluctuations of real GDP around its long-term trend, through phases of expansion, peak, contraction and trough.
Recession :: A fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters.
Potential output :: The level of real GDP the economy can produce when all resources are fully employed; the long-term trend.
Output gap :: The difference between actual and potential real GDP; positive (inflationary) when actual is above potential, negative (recessionary) when below.
@end
{{dia:business-cycle}}
| Phase | Real GDP | Unemployment | Inflation |
| **Expansion (recovery)** | Rising | Falling | Starting to rise |
| **Peak (boom)** | Above trend, growth slows | Lowest, below natural rate | High; shortages of labour and materials |
| **Contraction (recession)** | Falling | Rising | Falling |
| **Trough** | Lowest point, below trend | Highest | Low; possible deflation |

Real-world cycles are irregular: the 2008–09 financial crisis, the 2020 Covid recession (UK GDP fell about 10% in 2020) and the rapid rebound in 2021.

:::tip
Evaluate the model: phases are not regular or predictable, components of AD do not move together (consumption often recovers before investment), and a long recession can permanently lower the trend (**hysteresis**).
:::

## 3.2 | Variations in economic activity: aggregate demand and aggregate supply | SL | AD, SRAS, the new classical and Keynesian models of AS, equilibrium, output gaps and long-run growth.

### ad | Aggregate demand (AD)
@terms
Aggregate demand (AD) :: The total planned spending on an economy's goods and services at each price level in a given period: C + I + G + (X − M).
Wealth effect :: A fall in the price level raises the real value of household wealth, so consumption rises.
Interest rate effect :: A fall in the price level reduces demand for money, lowering interest rates and raising consumption and investment.
International trade effect :: A fall in the price level makes domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign goods, raising exports and reducing imports.
Consumer confidence :: How optimistic households are about their future incomes and the economy.
Business confidence :: How optimistic firms are about future sales and profits, a key driver of investment.
@end
{{dia:ad-curve}}
**Why AD slopes down**: the wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the international trade effect.

#### Determinants of each component (shift AD)
| Component | Typical share | Increases when… |
| **C** (consumption) | 50–70% | Consumer confidence rises; wealth rises (house and share prices); interest rates fall; income taxes fall; household debt is low; expectations of future price rises |
| **I** (investment) | 15–25% | Interest rates fall; business confidence rises; technology creates opportunities; corporate taxes fall; demand for output grows (accelerator); credit is easier to get |
| **G** (government spending) | 15–25% | Political priorities; expansionary fiscal policy; recession (automatic stabilisers) |
| **X − M** (net exports) | ± | Foreign incomes rise; the currency depreciates; trade barriers abroad fall; domestic incomes fall (fewer imports) |

### sras | Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)
@terms
Aggregate supply (AS) :: The total output that firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level in a given period.
Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) :: Aggregate supply in the period when wages and other resource prices are fixed; slopes upward.
Supply shock :: An unexpected event that shifts SRAS, such as a sudden rise in oil prices or a natural disaster.
@end
{{dia:sras}}
In the short run, money wages and other input prices are fixed by contracts, so a rise in the price level raises firms' profits and they expand output: **SRAS slopes up**.

**Shifts of SRAS** (changes in costs of production):
- **Wages** (union bargaining, minimum wage rises)
- **Prices of raw materials and energy** (oil shocks in 1973, 1979, 2022)
- **Import prices** (a depreciation raises the cost of imported inputs)
- **Indirect taxes and business taxes** (VAT rises shift SRAS left), **subsidies** (shift right)
- **Supply shocks** (war, pandemics, extreme weather, supply chain disruptions such as Covid-era shipping delays)

### as-models | Long-run aggregate supply: new classical and Keynesian views
@terms
Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) :: Aggregate supply when all wages and prices are fully flexible; vertical at potential output in the new classical model.
Full employment level of output (Yf) :: The output produced when unemployment is at its natural rate; potential output.
Keynesian AS curve :: An aggregate supply curve that is horizontal at low output (spare capacity), upward-sloping as the economy nears full employment, and vertical at full employment.
@end
#### New classical (monetarist) view
{{dia:nc-lras}}
In the long run, wages and prices adjust fully, so output returns to **Yf** whatever the level of AD. LRAS is **vertical**: potential output depends only on the quantity and quality of factors of production and technology.

#### Keynesian view
{{dia:keynes-as}}
Wages and prices are **sticky downward**, so the economy can be stuck below full employment for a long time. With large spare capacity, more AD raises output without raising prices (horizontal section). Near full capacity, bottlenecks push prices up. At Yf, more AD only raises prices.

:::eval New classical implications | Keynesian implications
+ The economy self-corrects; policy should avoid disturbing it.
+ Demand-side policy only changes the price level in the long run.
+ Focus on supply-side policies to raise Yf.
- Recessions can last a long time (the Great Depression, the 2010s in Europe).
- Expansionary demand-side policy raises output and employment when there is spare capacity.
- "In the long run we are all dead" (Keynes): waiting for self-correction is costly.
:::

### macro-equilibrium | Macroeconomic equilibrium and output gaps
@terms
Short-run equilibrium :: The output and price level where AD = SRAS.
Long-run equilibrium :: When AD, SRAS and LRAS all intersect at potential output.
Recessionary (deflationary) gap :: When equilibrium real GDP is below potential output, with cyclical unemployment.
Inflationary gap :: When equilibrium real GDP is above potential output, putting upward pressure on prices.
@end
{{dia:nc-gaps}}
#### New classical self-correction
- **From a recessionary gap**: unemployment pushes money wages down → costs fall → SRAS shifts right → output returns to Yf at a lower price level.
- **From an inflationary gap**: labour shortages push wages up → SRAS shifts left → output returns to Yf at a higher price level.

#### Keynesian equilibrium
The economy can settle at any output on the AS curve. A recessionary gap persists because wages do not fall easily (contracts, unions, minimum wages, morale) and falling incomes reduce spending further. The solution is to **raise AD**.

:::warn
An **inflationary gap** is about **output** being above potential; it is not the same as inflation itself. Say what happens to real GDP and to unemployment as well as the price level.
:::

### lras-shifts | Long-term growth: factors that shift LRAS
@terms
Productivity :: Output per unit of input, e.g. output per worker per hour.
Institutional change :: Changes in the rules of the economy, such as property rights, the legal system and the reduction of corruption.
@end
{{dia:lras-shift}}
| Factor | Example |
| **Quantity of factors** | Immigration, higher labour force participation (more women in work), discovery of resources, investment in capital |
| **Quality of factors** | Education and training (human capital), better health, better capital equipment |
| **Technology** | Automation, AI, the internet, improvements in agriculture |
| **Efficiency and institutions** | Stronger property rights, less corruption, competition, deregulation, better infrastructure |
| **Reductions in the natural rate of unemployment** | Better job matching, retraining, labour market flexibility |

:::tip
SRAS factors (costs) shift SRAS; LRAS factors (capacity) shift LRAS and the PPC. Do not explain a rise in oil prices as a shift in LRAS.
:::

## 3.3 | Macroeconomic objectives | SL | Growth, unemployment, inflation and deflation, conflicts between objectives (HL: Phillips curves), and sustainable government debt.

### growth | Economic growth
@terms
Economic growth :: An increase in real GDP (or real GDP per capita) over time, usually measured as an annual percentage change.
Short-term growth :: Growth from using existing resources more fully, caused by higher AD; a move towards the PPC.
Long-term growth :: Growth in potential output, caused by an increase in the quantity or quality of factors of production; an outward shift of the PPC and LRAS.
@end
:::formula Growth rate
Growth rate = \frac{real GDP_{year 2} − real GDP_{year 1}}{real GDP_{year 1}} × 100
:::
- **Short-term growth**: AD rises (e.g. a fall in interest rates), the economy moves towards full capacity: on an AD–AS diagram, a rightward shift of AD along SRAS; on a PPC, a move from inside to the curve.
- **Long-term growth**: LRAS shifts right; the PPC shifts out.

#### Consequences
| Positive | Negative |
| Higher incomes and living standards; more jobs | Inflation if AD outpaces AS |
| More tax revenue for public services without raising tax rates | **Environmental damage**: pollution, resource depletion, climate change |
| Lower poverty (absolute) | **Inequality** can widen if gains go to the top |
| Firms invest more; virtuous cycle | Current account deficits as imports rise |
| | Stress, longer working hours, loss of traditional ways of life |

:::tip
A rise in the growth rate does not automatically mean inflation: if LRAS shifts right, the economy can grow with stable or falling prices.
:::

### unemployment | Unemployment: measurement, types and costs
@terms
Unemployment :: People of working age who are without a job, available for work and actively seeking work.
Unemployment rate :: The number of unemployed people as a percentage of the labour force.
Labour force :: All people of working age who are either employed or unemployed (actively seeking work).
Underemployment :: When people work fewer hours than they want, or in jobs below their skill level.
Hidden unemployment :: People who want work but are not counted as unemployed, e.g. discouraged workers who have stopped looking or people on disability benefits.
Frictional unemployment :: Short-term unemployment of people between jobs.
Seasonal unemployment :: Unemployment caused by seasonal changes in demand, e.g. ski instructors in summer.
Structural unemployment :: Long-term unemployment caused by changes in the structure of the economy (demand, technology, location), leaving workers with the wrong skills or in the wrong place.
Cyclical unemployment :: Unemployment caused by a fall in aggregate demand during a downturn (demand-deficient unemployment).
Natural rate of unemployment (NRU) :: The rate of unemployment when the economy is at full employment: frictional + structural + seasonal unemployment.
@end
:::formula Unemployment rate
Unemployment rate = \frac{number unemployed}{labour force} × 100
Labour force = employed + unemployed
:::
:::calc Unemployment rate
Employed = 31.2m, unemployed = 1.3m. Labour force = 32.5m. Rate = 1.3 ÷ 32.5 × 100 = **4%**.
:::
**Measurement problems**: hidden unemployment (discouraged workers), underemployment (part-time workers wanting full-time), the informal economy, regional and group disparities hidden by averages (youth unemployment in Spain above 25%), and differences between the claimant count and the ILO labour force survey.

#### Types and causes
| Type | Cause | Policy |
| **Frictional** | Time spent searching between jobs | Better information: job centres, online matching |
| **Seasonal** | Demand varies by season | Hard to remove; diversify the local economy |
| **Structural** | Changing demand, technology, globalisation; occupational and geographical immobility | Retraining, education, relocation help, regional policy |
| **Cyclical** | Fall in AD | Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy |
| **Real-wage** | Wages held above equilibrium | Labour market reforms (contested) |

{{dia:structural-unemp}}
{{dia:real-wage-unemp}}

#### Costs of unemployment
- **Individuals**: lost income, stress and poor health, loss of skills (making future jobs harder), family breakdown.
- **Society**: crime, social exclusion, regional decline.
- **Economy**: lost output (inside the PPC), lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending (worse budget balance), greater inequality and poverty, possible **hysteresis** (long-term unemployment raises the natural rate).

### inflation | Inflation, disinflation and deflation
@terms
Inflation :: A sustained increase in the average price level in an economy over a period of time.
Disinflation :: A fall in the rate of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly.
Deflation :: A sustained decrease in the average price level in an economy.
Consumer price index (CPI) :: An index of the average prices of a representative basket of goods and services bought by a typical household, weighted by their share in spending.
Demand-pull inflation :: Inflation caused by aggregate demand growing faster than aggregate supply.
Cost-push inflation :: Inflation caused by rising costs of production shifting SRAS left.
Stagflation :: Rising inflation combined with stagnant or falling output and rising unemployment.
Core inflation :: An inflation measure that excludes volatile items such as food and energy.
Producer price index (PPI) :: An index measuring changes in the prices firms pay for inputs or receive for output; an early signal of consumer price inflation.
@end
#### Measuring inflation
A **basket** of typical goods and services is priced each month; each item is **weighted** by its share of household spending. The CPI compares the cost of the basket with a base year (= 100).

:::formula Inflation from a price index
Inflation rate = \frac{CPI_{this year} − CPI_{last year}}{CPI_{last year}} × 100
Price index = \frac{cost of basket in current year}{cost of basket in base year} × 100
:::
:::hl Constructing a weighted price index
| Item | Weight | Price index (base = 100) | Weight × index |
| Food | 0.30 | 108 | 32.4 |
| Housing | 0.40 | 104 | 41.6 |
| Transport | 0.20 | 95 | 19.0 |
| Other | 0.10 | 110 | 11.0 |
| **Total** | 1.00 | | **104.0** |
Weighted index = 104.0 → prices have risen by **4%** since the base year. Steps: (1) find each item's price index, (2) multiply by its weight, (3) add.
:::

**Limitations of the CPI**: the basket may not match individual households (pensioners, students); weights are out of date as habits change; quality improvements and new products are hard to capture; substitution bias (people switch to cheaper goods); differences between regions and income groups. Economists also watch **core inflation** and the **PPI**.

#### Causes
{{dia:inflation-types}}

#### Costs of inflation
- **Reduces purchasing power**, especially for people on fixed incomes (pensioners, benefits not index-linked).
- **Uncertainty**: firms delay investment.
- **Redistribution**: from lenders and savers to borrowers when real interest rates are negative.
- **International competitiveness**: exports become dearer than rivals' (if inflation is higher than abroad).
- **Menu and shoe-leather costs**; **fiscal drag** (people pushed into higher tax brackets).
- **Inflationary expectations** can become self-fulfilling (wage-price spiral).
- Very high inflation (hyperinflation in Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela 2018) destroys the use of money.

A **low, stable** rate (central bank targets around **2%**) is better than zero: it gives a buffer against deflation, lets real wages adjust and reflects quality improvements that the CPI misses.

#### Deflation
{{dia:deflation-types}}
**Costs of (demand-side) deflation**: consumers delay purchases (expecting lower prices), the real value of debt rises (debt deflation), firms cut output and jobs, real interest rates rise even at zero nominal rates, and monetary policy becomes ineffective (Japan, 1990s–2010s).

:::tip
Always state the **size** and the **cause**. 2% inflation is a target, not a problem. Cost-push and demand-pull need different policies; supply-side deflation can be a good thing.
:::

### conflicts | Conflicts between macroeconomic objectives
@terms
Phillips curve :: A curve showing an inverse relationship between the inflation rate and the unemployment rate in the short run.
Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC) :: A downward-sloping curve showing a trade-off between inflation and unemployment for a given level of expected inflation.
Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) :: A vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment, showing no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.
Expectations-augmented Phillips curve :: The idea that the SRPC shifts up when people expect higher inflation, so attempts to keep unemployment below the NRU only raise inflation.
@end
| Conflict | Explanation |
| **Low unemployment vs low inflation** | Rising AD cuts unemployment but raises inflation (demand-pull) |
| **Growth vs environment** | Growth raises output, pollution and resource use (sustainability) |
| **Growth vs current account** | Rising incomes raise imports, worsening the current account |
| **Growth vs equity** | Growth may benefit capital owners and high-skilled workers most |
| **Low inflation vs growth** | Contractionary policy to cut inflation slows growth (the 2022–23 rate rises) |

:::hl Short-run and long-run Phillips curves
{{dia:phillips}}
**The SRPC** (A. W. Phillips, 1958, UK data 1861–1957) suggested governments could choose a combination of inflation and unemployment.
**Stagflation in the 1970s** (oil shocks: high inflation and high unemployment together) broke the stable relationship.
**The expectations-augmented view** (Friedman, Phelps): if the government expands AD to push unemployment below the NRU (A → B), workers soon expect higher inflation and demand higher wages; SRAS shifts left and unemployment returns to the NRU at a higher inflation rate (B → C). The **LRPC is vertical** at the NRU. The only way to lower unemployment permanently is to reduce the NRU through **supply-side policies**.
The reverse also applies: a contractionary policy raises unemployment above the NRU in the short run; as expectations of inflation fall, the SRPC shifts down and unemployment returns to the NRU at lower inflation.
:::

### govt-debt | Sustainable levels of government debt
@terms
Budget deficit :: When government spending is greater than government revenue in a year.
Budget surplus :: When government revenue is greater than government spending in a year.
Government (national) debt :: The total amount the government owes: the accumulation of past budget deficits minus surpluses.
Debt-to-GDP ratio :: Government debt as a percentage of GDP; a measure of the sustainability of debt.
Debt servicing :: The interest payments a government must make on its debt.
Credit rating :: An assessment by agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) of a borrower's ability to repay; a lower rating raises borrowing costs.
@end
A **deficit** is a flow (one year); the **debt** is a stock (accumulated). Running deficits adds to debt.

| Country (approx., 2024) | Debt as % of GDP |
| Japan | ~250% |
| Italy | ~135% |
| USA | ~120% |
| UK | ~100% |
| Germany | ~63% |
| Saudi Arabia | ~26% |

#### Why debt can be a problem
- **Debt servicing costs** have an opportunity cost: interest paid instead of schools and hospitals.
- Higher debt may raise interest rates (crowding out of private investment).
- **Credit rating downgrades** raise the cost of borrowing (Greece 2010–12; UK gilt crisis after the September 2022 mini-budget).
- Future generations face **higher taxes** or spending cuts.
- Debt in foreign currency is especially risky for developing economies (Sri Lanka's default in 2022; Zambia 2020).
- Less room for fiscal policy in the next recession.

**When is debt sustainable?** When the economy grows faster than the interest rate on debt (r < g), the debt-to-GDP ratio can fall even with small deficits; when debt is held domestically in the country's own currency (Japan); and when markets trust the government.

## 3.4 | Economics of inequality and poverty | SL | Measuring inequality and poverty, their causes and costs, and policies including taxation and transfers.

### measuring-inequality | Measuring inequality and poverty
@terms
Income inequality :: Unequal distribution of income among individuals or households.
Wealth inequality :: Unequal distribution of assets (property, shares, savings) among individuals or households; usually greater than income inequality.
Lorenz curve :: A graph showing the cumulative percentage of income earned by the cumulative percentage of the population, ranked from poorest to richest.
Gini coefficient :: A measure of inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality), equal to the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve divided by the total area under the line of equality.
Absolute poverty :: Income below the level needed to meet basic needs; the World Bank's international poverty line is $3.00 a day (2021 PPP, updated in 2025).
Relative poverty :: Income below a set proportion of the typical income in a country, e.g. below 60% of median income.
Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) :: A UNDP/Oxford measure of poverty using ten indicators across health, education and living standards.
@end
{{dia:lorenz}}
#### Constructing a Lorenz curve from quintile data
| Quintile | Share of income (%) | Cumulative (%) |
| Poorest 20% | 4 | 4 |
| Second 20% | 9 | 13 |
| Third 20% | 14 | 27 |
| Fourth 20% | 22 | 49 |
| Richest 20% | 51 | 100 |
Plot (20, 4), (40, 13), (60, 27), (80, 49), (100, 100). A country whose curve lies closer to the diagonal is more equal.

**Gini examples (income, approx.)**: Slovakia 0.24, Norway 0.27, UK 0.33, USA 0.39–0.41, Brazil 0.52, South Africa 0.63 (the world's highest).

#### Measuring poverty
- **Absolute poverty line**: the World Bank's **$3.00 a day** (2021 PPP), updated from $2.15 in 2025; about 800 million people lived below it.
- **Relative poverty**: used in rich countries (60% of median income).
- **MPI**: 10 indicators: nutrition, child mortality (health); years of schooling, school attendance (education); cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets (living standards). Someone deprived in a third or more of the weighted indicators is multidimensionally poor.

**Difficulties**: choice of poverty line is arbitrary; differences in cost of living; informal income; data collection in conflict zones and remote areas; household surveys miss inequality within households.

### inequality-causes | Causes and consequences of inequality and poverty
@terms
Social mobility :: The ability of people to move between social or income groups, e.g. children earning more than their parents.
Intergenerational poverty :: Poverty passed from one generation to the next.
@end
#### Causes
- **Inequality of opportunity**: unequal access to education, healthcare and finance.
- **Different levels of human capital**; **discrimination** (gender pay gap, caste, ethnicity).
- **Unequal wealth ownership and inheritance**; returns on wealth exceed wage growth.
- **Technological change and globalisation**: rewards skills; low-skill jobs automated or offshored.
- **Tax and benefit systems**: regressive taxes, limited welfare.
- **Market power** of firms and declining union power.
- **Geography**: rural vs urban, regional decline.
- **Poverty traps** ([[e-barriers|4.9]]).

#### Consequences
- **Lower economic growth** (the poor cannot invest in education; IMF research links high inequality to slower, less durable growth).
- **Lower social mobility**, intergenerational poverty.
- **Social problems**: crime, poorer health and shorter lives, lower trust, political instability.
- **Unequal political power**; populism.
- **Lower aggregate demand** (the rich have a lower MPC).

### tax-policy | Taxation to reduce inequality
@terms
Direct tax :: A tax on income or wealth paid directly to the government by the person or firm, e.g. income tax, corporation tax, wealth tax.
Progressive tax :: A tax where the average tax rate rises as income rises, so higher earners pay a larger proportion of their income.
Proportional tax :: A tax where the average tax rate is the same at all incomes (a flat tax).
Regressive tax :: A tax where the average tax rate falls as income rises, so lower earners pay a larger proportion of their income.
Average tax rate :: Total tax paid divided by total income.
Marginal tax rate :: The tax rate paid on the last (extra) dollar of income.
Wealth tax :: A tax on the value of assets owned.
Capital gains tax :: A tax on the profit from selling an asset that has risen in value.
@end
{{dia:tax-systems}}
| Tax | Type | Example |
| Personal income tax | Direct, progressive | UK 0% / 20% / 40% / 45% bands |
| Corporation tax | Direct | UK 25%, Ireland 12.5% → 15% minimum (OECD global deal) |
| VAT / GST / sales taxes | Indirect, regressive in effect | UK VAT 20%, Singapore GST 9% |
| Excise taxes | Indirect, regressive | Tobacco, alcohol, fuel |
| Wealth, property, inheritance, capital gains taxes | Direct | Norway, Spain, Switzerland have wealth taxes |

:::hl Average and marginal tax rate calculation
Tax bands: 0% on the first $10,000; 20% on $10,001–$40,000; 40% above $40,000. Income = $55,000.
Tax = 0 + 20% × 30,000 + 40% × 15,000 = 6,000 + 6,000 = **$12,000**.
Average tax rate = 12,000 ÷ 55,000 × 100 = **21.82%**. Marginal tax rate = **40%**.
In a progressive system the marginal rate is above the average rate.
:::

:::eval Progressive taxes | Concerns
+ Reduce inequality; fund transfers and public services.
+ Based on ability to pay (vertical equity).
- High marginal rates may reduce incentives to work, save and invest.
- Encourage tax avoidance and evasion; skilled workers and capital may move abroad.
- The **Laffer curve** argument: very high rates may raise less revenue.
:::

### other-policies | Other policies to reduce inequality and poverty
@terms
Transfer payments :: Payments from the government to individuals for which no good or service is produced in return, e.g. pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefits.
Universal basic income (UBI) :: A regular, unconditional cash payment from the government to every adult, regardless of income or employment.
Means-tested benefit :: A benefit paid only to people whose income or wealth is below a certain level.
Conditional cash transfer :: Cash given to poor households on condition that they meet requirements, such as keeping children in school or attending health check-ups.
@end
| Policy | Examples | Evaluation |
| **Investment in human capital** (education, health) | Free schooling, school meals, public healthcare | Long-run, tackles causes; costly and slow |
| **Transfer payments** | Pensions, unemployment and child benefits | Immediate; may reduce work incentives (poverty trap); cost |
| **Conditional cash transfers** | Brazil's Bolsa Família; Mexico's Prospera | Raise school attendance and health; administrative costs |
| **Universal basic income** | Pilots in Finland (2017–18), Kenya (GiveDirectly) | Simple, no stigma; very costly; may reduce work |
| **Minimum wage** | UK National Living Wage, Germany (2015) | Raises low pay; possible job losses |
| **Labour market and anti-discrimination policies** | Equal pay laws, quotas, affirmative action | Address discrimination; enforcement |
| **Policies for wealth** | Inheritance tax, property tax, wealth tax | Target wealth inequality; avoidance, capital flight |
| **Universal public services** | Free healthcare (NHS), subsidised housing and transport | "Social wage" benefits low-income households most |

:::tip
Equity vs efficiency is the classic trade-off: redistribution improves equity but may reduce incentives. Evaluate with evidence: Nordic countries combine high taxes, low inequality and high productivity.
:::

## 3.5 | Demand management (demand-side policies): monetary policy | SL | Central banks, interest rates, the money market, tools (including QE), and the strengths and limits of monetary policy.

### monetary-overview | Goals of monetary policy and how it works
@terms
Monetary policy :: The use of interest rates, the money supply and credit conditions by a central bank to influence aggregate demand and achieve macroeconomic objectives.
Central bank :: The institution responsible for a country's monetary policy, e.g. the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England.
Inflation targeting :: A policy in which the central bank publicly commits to keeping inflation at a target rate, usually around 2%.
Expansionary monetary policy :: Lowering interest rates or increasing the money supply to raise AD (also called loose monetary policy).
Contractionary monetary policy :: Raising interest rates or reducing the money supply to reduce AD (also called tight monetary policy).
Nominal interest rate :: The interest rate stated on a loan or deposit, not adjusted for inflation.
Real interest rate :: The nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation.
Money supply :: The total amount of money (cash and bank deposits) in an economy.
@end
**Goals**: low and stable inflation (usually a **2% target**: Bank of England, ECB "2% over the medium term", Fed), low unemployment, stable economic growth, a stable exchange rate, and, in some countries, financial stability.

:::formula Real interest rate
Real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate
:::
:::calc Real interest rate
In 2022 the UK base rate reached 3.5% while CPI inflation was about 10.5%: real rate ≈ 3.5 − 10.5 = **−7%**. Savers lost purchasing power and borrowing was very cheap in real terms.
:::

#### Interest rate determination: the money market
{{dia:money-market}}
#### Transmission: how a rate cut raises AD
1. Borrowing becomes cheaper → **consumption** on credit (cars, houses) and **investment** rise.
2. Saving is less attractive → consumption rises.
3. Mortgage payments fall → disposable income rises.
4. Asset prices (houses, shares) rise → **wealth effect**.
5. The currency tends to **depreciate** (hot money leaves) → exports cheaper, imports dearer → **(X − M)** rises.
{{dia:ad-curve}}
Contractionary policy works in reverse: the 2022–23 rate rises (Fed to 5.25–5.5%, Bank of England to 5.25%) were aimed at the post-pandemic inflation surge.

### monetary-tools | Tools of monetary policy and money creation
@terms
Commercial bank :: A private bank that accepts deposits and makes loans.
Fractional reserve banking :: A system in which banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest, creating new money.
Reserve requirement :: The minimum percentage of deposits a commercial bank must hold as reserves with the central bank.
Open market operations :: The buying and selling of government bonds by the central bank to change the money supply and interest rates.
Base rate (policy rate) :: The interest rate the central bank charges commercial banks on loans, which influences all other interest rates.
Quantitative easing (QE) :: When a central bank creates new money electronically to buy large quantities of financial assets, such as government bonds, to increase the money supply and lower long-term interest rates.
@end
#### How commercial banks create money
With a 10% reserve requirement, a $1,000 deposit lets the bank lend $900; that $900 is deposited in another bank, which lends $810, and so on. The maximum total new deposits = initial deposit × **1 ÷ reserve ratio** = 1,000 × 10 = **$10,000**. In practice banks hold excess reserves and lending depends on demand, so the money multiplier is smaller.

| Tool | Expansionary | Contractionary |
| **Base (policy) rate** | Cut the rate; commercial rates follow | Raise the rate |
| **Open market operations** | Central bank **buys** bonds → banks' reserves rise → more lending | Central bank **sells** bonds |
| **Reserve requirements** | Lower the ratio → banks can lend more | Raise the ratio (China's PBoC uses this actively) |
| **Quantitative easing (QE)** | Create money to buy bonds on a massive scale → bond prices up, long-term yields down | **Quantitative tightening** (QT): selling bonds or not replacing them |

QE was used after 2008 and in 2020 when rates were already near zero (Fed, ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Japan). Critics argue it inflated asset prices and wealth inequality.

:::tip
QE and open market operations look similar. The difference: in QE the central bank **creates new money** to buy assets on a very large scale when interest rates are already near zero.
:::

### monetary-eval | Evaluation of monetary policy
@terms
Zero lower bound :: The limit on cutting nominal interest rates much below zero, which restricts conventional monetary policy in a deep recession.
Liquidity trap :: A situation where interest rates are so low that further cuts or increases in the money supply fail to raise spending.
Central bank independence :: When the central bank sets monetary policy free from political control.
@end
:::eval Strengths | Weaknesses
+ **Independent** central banks avoid political short-termism; credible inflation targets anchor expectations.
+ **Flexible and fast**: rates can be changed at each meeting (8 times a year).
+ Small, incremental changes; no direct effect on the budget deficit.
+ Effective at reducing demand-pull inflation.
- **Time lags**: 18–24 months for the full effect.
- **Zero lower bound / liquidity trap**: in a deep recession, low rates may not revive spending if confidence is weak (Japan; 2009).
- Weak against **cost-push** inflation: higher rates cut output further.
- Blunt instrument: affects all regions and sectors (hurts mortgage holders, helps savers).
- Depends on banks passing rates on and being willing to lend.
- Exchange rate effects may conflict with other goals.
:::

## 3.6 | Demand management: fiscal policy | SL | Government revenue and spending, expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy, automatic stabilisers, the multiplier (HL) and the limits of fiscal policy.

### fiscal-overview | Fiscal policy: revenue, spending and goals
@terms
Fiscal policy :: The use of government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand and achieve macroeconomic objectives.
Current expenditure :: Government spending on day-to-day items, such as wages of public sector workers and supplies.
Capital expenditure :: Government spending on long-term investment, such as roads, schools and hospitals.
Expansionary fiscal policy :: Increasing government spending and/or cutting taxes to raise AD.
Contractionary fiscal policy :: Cutting government spending and/or raising taxes to reduce AD.
Automatic stabilisers :: Changes in tax revenue and government spending that happen automatically with the business cycle and reduce its fluctuations, e.g. rising unemployment benefits and falling tax revenue in a recession.
Discretionary fiscal policy :: Deliberate changes in government spending or tax rates.
Austerity :: Sustained cuts in government spending and/or tax increases to reduce a budget deficit.
@end
#### Sources of government revenue
Direct taxes (income, corporation, wealth); indirect taxes (VAT, excise); sale of goods and services (state-owned enterprises, fees); sale of government assets (privatisation).

#### Types of government spending
**Current** (salaries, supplies), **capital** (infrastructure, schools) and **transfer payments** (pensions, benefits; not counted in G for GDP).

#### Goals
Stable growth and low unemployment; low inflation; equitable income distribution; stable business environment; promote potential growth (capital spending).

| Expansionary (to close a recessionary gap) | Contractionary (to close an inflationary gap) |
| ↑ G (infrastructure, public services) | ↓ G |
| ↓ income tax → ↑ disposable income → ↑ C | ↑ income tax → ↓ C |
| ↓ corporation tax → ↑ I | ↑ corporation tax |
| AD shifts right: real GDP ↑, unemployment ↓, price level ↑ | AD shifts left: inflation ↓, but unemployment ↑ |

**Automatic stabilisers**: in a recession, tax revenues fall and unemployment benefits rise automatically, cushioning AD without any new decision. In a boom they work the other way.

:::ex Covid-19 response
In 2020 governments ran huge deficits: the US CARES Act ($2.2tn) and later packages; the UK furlough scheme paid up to 80% of wages for 11 million jobs. Budget deficits rose to around 15% of GDP in the US and the UK, preventing a deeper collapse in AD.
:::

### multiplier | The Keynesian multiplier | HL
@terms
Multiplier :: The ratio of the final change in real GDP to the initial change in an injection: an injection causes a larger final change in national income.
Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) :: The proportion of an extra dollar of income that is spent on consumption.
Marginal propensity to save (MPS) :: The proportion of an extra dollar of income that is saved.
Marginal propensity to tax (MPT) :: The proportion of an extra dollar of income that is paid in tax.
Marginal propensity to import (MPM) :: The proportion of an extra dollar of income that is spent on imports.
Marginal propensity to withdraw (MPW) :: MPS + MPT + MPM: the proportion of extra income that leaks out of the circular flow.
@end
{{dia:multiplier}}
:::formula The multiplier
k = \frac{1}{1 − MPC} = \frac{1}{MPW}
MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM :: and MPC + MPW = 1 (for domestic spending)
ΔY = k × ΔJ :: where ΔJ is the change in an injection (I, G or X)
:::
:::calc Multiplier
MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.20, MPM = 0.15 → MPW = 0.5 → k = 1 ÷ 0.5 = **2**. A $10bn rise in G raises real GDP by up to **$20bn**.
**Closing a gap**: an economy has a $22bn recessionary gap and MPC = 0.6. k = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5. Required ΔG = 22 ÷ 2.5 = **$8.8bn**.
:::
**Significance**: a small injection can have a large effect, which strengthens the case for fiscal policy. But the multiplier is smaller in open economies with high imports (MPM), high tax rates and high saving; it takes time (lags); and the value is uncertain, so governments can over- or under-shoot. Multipliers on infrastructure spending in a recession tend to be higher than on tax cuts.

### fiscal-eval | Evaluation of fiscal policy
@terms
Crowding out :: When increased government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private sector investment and consumption.
Time lags :: Delays between identifying a problem, deciding on a policy, implementing it and the policy having its full effect.
@end
:::eval Strengths | Weaknesses
+ **Effective in deep recessions** when monetary policy is stuck at the zero lower bound.
+ Can **target** specific sectors, regions and groups (equity).
+ Capital spending raises **potential output** (supply-side effect).
+ Automatic stabilisers act immediately.
+ The multiplier magnifies its effect.
- **Time lags**: recognition, decision (political process, annual budget), implementation (infrastructure takes years).
- **Crowding out** of private investment (new classical view).
- **Political pressures**: hard to reverse spending or raise taxes; spending timed for elections.
- **Sustainability of debt**: high debt limits room for expansion; markets may react badly (UK 2022).
- Possible inflation if the economy is near full capacity.
- Ricardian equivalence: people may save tax cuts expecting future tax rises.
:::
{{dia:crowding-out}}

## 3.7 | Supply-side policies | SL | Market-based and interventionist policies to raise potential output, their demand-side effects and an evaluation.

### supply-side | Market-based and interventionist supply-side policies
@terms
Supply-side policies :: Policies designed to increase the productive capacity (potential output) of the economy by improving the quantity and quality of factors of production and the efficiency of markets.
Market-based supply-side policies :: Policies that reduce government intervention and increase competition and incentives, e.g. deregulation, privatisation, tax cuts, labour market reforms.
Interventionist supply-side policies :: Policies in which the government actively invests or intervenes to raise potential output, e.g. education, healthcare, infrastructure, R&D and industrial policy.
Privatisation :: The transfer of ownership of a firm or industry from the public sector to the private sector.
Deregulation :: The removal of rules and regulations, to increase competition and reduce costs.
Labour market flexibility :: The ease with which employers can hire, fire and change working conditions, and with which wages adjust.
Industrial policy :: Government policies to support particular industries, e.g. subsidies, tax credits and targeted investment.
@end
**Goals**: long-term growth (shift LRAS right), lower natural unemployment, lower inflationary pressure, improved international competitiveness, and higher productivity.
{{dia:lras-shift}}
| Market-based | Interventionist |
| **Competition policy**: anti-monopoly law, deregulation of markets (airlines, telecoms) | **Investment in human capital**: education, training, apprenticeships, healthcare |
| **Privatisation** (UK 1980s: BT, British Gas; airport and rail privatisations elsewhere) | **Investment in infrastructure**: transport, broadband, energy (China's high-speed rail; India's highways) |
| **Labour market reforms**: weaker union power, lower or no minimum wage, cuts in unemployment benefits, easier hiring and firing | **Investment in R&D and new technology**: grants, tax credits, research funding |
| **Incentive-related tax cuts**: lower income tax (work incentives), lower corporation tax (investment) | **Industrial policies**: support for strategic sectors (US CHIPS and Science Act 2022, Inflation Reduction Act 2022; EU Green Deal Industrial Plan) |
| **Trade liberalisation**: lower tariffs, more competition | **Labour market policies**: job centres, retraining, childcare to raise participation |

:::tip
Essays often ask you to compare the two types. Remember private firms also invest to raise capacity because of the profit incentive; supply-side policy is not only government's job.
:::

### supply-side-eval | Effectiveness of supply-side policies
@terms
Productivity gap :: The difference in output per worker or per hour between countries or regions.
@end
#### Demand-side effects of supply-side policies
Many supply-side policies also raise AD in the short run: infrastructure spending raises G; tax cuts raise C and I; education spending raises G. So they can shift both AD and LRAS.

#### Supply-side effects of fiscal policy
Capital spending on infrastructure, education and health raises potential output; tax incentives for investment raise the capital stock. The line between fiscal policy and supply-side policy is not sharp.

:::eval Strengths | Weaknesses
+ Raise potential output: growth **without** inflation.
+ Lower the natural rate of unemployment; reduce structural unemployment.
+ Improve international competitiveness and the current account in the long run.
+ Market-based policies often have low direct cost.
- **Long time lags**: education and infrastructure take years to pay off.
- **Costly** for government budgets (interventionist).
- **Equity concerns**: tax cuts favour high earners; weaker labour protection increases inequality and job insecurity.
- **Environmental costs** of growth and deregulation.
- Uncertain effects: tax cuts may not raise work effort (UK 2022 mini-budget showed market risks); privatised monopolies may still need regulation (UK water companies' sewage pollution and debt).
- Do not help in a recession caused by low AD.
:::
