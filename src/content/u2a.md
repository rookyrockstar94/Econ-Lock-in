## 2.1 | Demand | SL | The law of demand, what shifts the demand curve, and (HL) why demand slopes down and linear demand functions.

### demand-law | The law of demand and the demand curve
@terms
Demand :: The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price, in a given time period, ceteris paribus.
Effective demand :: Desire for a good backed by the ability to pay for it.
Law of demand :: There is an inverse relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.
Market demand :: The sum of all individual consumers' demand for a good at each price (horizontal summation).
Quantity demanded :: The amount consumers will buy at one particular price.
Extension in demand :: An increase in quantity demanded caused by a fall in the good's own price: a movement down the demand curve.
Contraction in demand :: A decrease in quantity demanded caused by a rise in the good's own price: a movement up the demand curve.
@end
Demand needs both **willingness and ability** to pay. A demand curve plots the quantity demanded at each price; economists draw it as a straight line for convenience.

**Market demand** is found by adding individual demands **horizontally**: if at $10 the boys' swimwear demand is 500 and girls' is 400, market demand at $10 is 900.

#### Movement along vs shift
{{dia:demand-move}}
A change in the **good's own price** causes a **movement along** the curve: a contraction (price up) or an extension (price down) in **quantity demanded**. Anything else that changes demand **shifts** the curve (see next note).

:::tip
Use precise language. Own-price change → "quantity demanded changes" (movement). Non-price determinant → "demand changes" (shift). Examiners deduct marks for mixing them up.
:::

### demand-determinants | Non-price determinants of demand
@terms
Normal good :: A good for which demand rises when income rises (positive YED).
Inferior good :: A good for which demand falls when income rises (negative YED), e.g. bus travel, discount supermarket brands.
Substitutes :: Goods that can be used in place of each other; a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other.
Complements :: Goods used together; a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other.
@end
{{dia:demand-shift}}
| Determinant | Increase in demand (shift right) when… | Example |
| **Income** | Income rises (normal good) or falls (inferior good) | Rising incomes in India raise demand for cars; fall in demand for bicycles |
| **Tastes and preferences** | Fashion, advertising or health information favour the good | Plant-based milk after health campaigns; demand for a product endorsed by an influencer |
| **Price of substitutes** | A substitute becomes dearer | Higher Coca-Cola price raises demand for Pepsi |
| **Price of complements** | A complement becomes cheaper | Cheaper games consoles raise demand for games |
| **Number of consumers** | Population grows, or market opens to new buyers | Ageing populations raise demand for healthcare |
| **Expectations of future prices** | Consumers expect prices to rise | Panic buying of fuel before an expected price rise |
| **Other** | Seasons, interest rates (for goods bought on credit), government policy | Demand for umbrellas in the monsoon |

:::warn
- Saying a change in price "shifts the demand curve".
- Forgetting that demand for an inferior good falls when income rises.
- Shifting supply when consumer tastes change; tastes affect demand only.
:::

### demand-hl | Why demand slopes down and linear demand functions | HL
@terms
Income effect :: When a good's price falls, consumers' real income (purchasing power) rises, so they can buy more of it.
Substitution effect :: When a good's price falls it becomes cheaper relative to substitutes, so consumers switch towards it.
Marginal utility :: The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good.
Law of diminishing marginal utility :: As a person consumes more units of a good, the extra satisfaction from each additional unit falls.
Linear demand function :: An equation of the form Qd = a − bP, where a is the quantity demanded at a price of zero and b shows how much Qd changes for each $1 change in price.
@end
#### Three explanations of the law of demand
1. **Income effect**: a lower price raises real income, so more is bought.
2. **Substitution effect**: a lower price makes the good relatively cheaper than its substitutes.
3. **Diminishing marginal utility**: each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so consumers will only buy more if the price falls. A hungry student gets great satisfaction from the first slice of pizza, less from the fourth.

#### Linear demand functions: Qd = a − bP
- **a** is the horizontal (Q-axis) intercept: quantity demanded when P = 0. A change in a non-price determinant changes **a** and **shifts** the curve (a rises → shift right).
- **b** is the slope parameter: each $1 rise in price reduces Qd by b units. A larger **b** makes the curve flatter on a P–Q diagram. A change in b **pivots** the curve.
- The price at which Qd = 0 (vertical intercept) is a ÷ b.

:::calc Plotting Qd = 60 − 4P
| Price ($) | 0 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 15 |
| Qd | 60 | 48 | 36 | 24 | 12 | 0 |
Price intercept: 60 ÷ 4 = $15. If incomes rise and the function becomes Qd = 80 − 4P, the curve shifts right by 20 units at every price, parallel to the old one.
:::

:::tip
Paper 3 often asks you to plot a demand function from a schedule, then shift it. Always label the intercepts and use a ruler.
:::

## 2.2 | Supply | SL | The law of supply, what shifts supply, and (HL) marginal cost and linear supply functions.

### supply-law | The law of supply and the supply curve
@terms
Supply :: The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price, in a given time period, ceteris paribus.
Law of supply :: There is a positive relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied, ceteris paribus.
Market supply :: The sum of all individual firms' supply at each price.
Extension in supply :: A rise in quantity supplied caused by a rise in the good's own price.
Contraction in supply :: A fall in quantity supplied caused by a fall in the good's own price.
@end
A higher price makes production more profitable and lets firms cover the higher costs of producing extra units, so quantity supplied rises. Market supply is the horizontal sum of all firms' supply.
{{dia:supply-move}}

### supply-determinants | Non-price determinants of supply
@terms
Joint supply :: When producing one good automatically produces another, e.g. beef and leather.
Competitive supply :: When resources used to produce one good could produce another instead, e.g. a farmer choosing wheat or barley.
Supply shock :: A sudden, unexpected change in supply, e.g. a drought or war.
@end
{{dia:supply-shift}}
| Determinant | Supply increases (shift right) when… | Example |
| **Costs of factors of production** | Wages, raw material or energy costs fall | Falling global cotton prices raise T-shirt supply |
| **Technology** | Better technology raises productivity | Automation in car plants; drip irrigation |
| **Indirect taxes** | Taxes are cut (taxes shift S left) | A fuel duty rise shifts fuel supply left |
| **Subsidies** | Subsidies are introduced (shift S right) | Subsidies for solar panels |
| **Prices of related goods** | Joint supply: the other good's price rises. Competitive supply: the other good's price falls | Higher beef prices raise leather supply |
| **Number of firms** | New firms enter | More cafés open in a city |
| **Expectations** | Firms expect future prices to fall (sell now) | Oil producers releasing stock before an expected fall |
| **Shocks** | Good harvests; absence of disasters | Drought in Brazil cuts coffee supply; war in Ukraine cut wheat exports in 2022 |

:::warn
A subsidy to producers shifts **supply** right, not demand. A tax on producers shifts **supply** left.
:::

### supply-hl | Marginal cost and linear supply functions | HL
@terms
Law of diminishing marginal returns :: In the short run, as more units of a variable factor (e.g. labour) are added to a fixed factor (e.g. capital), the extra output from each additional unit of the variable factor eventually falls.
Marginal cost (MC) :: The additional cost of producing one more unit of output.
Linear supply function :: An equation of the form Qs = c + dP, where c is the quantity supplied at a price of zero and d shows how much Qs changes for each $1 change in price.
@end
#### Why supply slopes upward
In the short run firms have fixed factors (a factory of a given size). Adding more workers eventually adds less and less output each (**diminishing marginal returns**), so each extra unit costs more to make (**increasing marginal cost**). Firms will only supply extra units if the price rises enough to cover that higher MC. The supply curve is the firm's **MC curve** (above minimum AVC; see [[e-perfect-competition|2.11]]).

#### Linear supply functions: Qs = c + dP
- **c** is the Q-intercept. If c is negative, the curve meets the price axis above the origin (at P = −c ÷ d). A change in a non-price determinant changes **c** (shift).
- **d** is the slope parameter: each $1 rise in price raises Qs by d units. A change in d **pivots** the curve.

:::calc Plotting Qs = −20 + 5P
At P = 4, Qs = 0 (price intercept $4). At P = 10, Qs = 30. At P = 16, Qs = 60. A technological improvement that makes the function Qs = 10 + 5P shifts supply right by 30 units at every price.
:::

## 2.3 | Competitive market equilibrium | SL | How the market clears, the price mechanism, consumer and producer surplus, and allocative efficiency.

### equilibrium | Equilibrium and disequilibrium
@terms
Market :: Any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to trade, physical or online.
Equilibrium :: A situation in which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change.
Excess demand (shortage) :: When quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at the current price.
Excess supply (surplus) :: When quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at the current price.
Competitive market :: A market with many buyers and sellers, none of whom can influence the price.
@end
{{dia:equilibrium}}
- **Above equilibrium** (P₁): a surplus; unsold stock builds up, firms cut prices, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until Qd = Qs.
- **Below equilibrium** (P₂): a shortage; buyers bid prices up, quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts.

#### Changes in equilibrium
{{dia:eq-demand-up}}
{{dia:eq-supply-up}}
| Change | Price | Quantity |
| Demand ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |
| Demand ↓ | ↓ | ↓ |
| Supply ↑ | ↓ | ↑ |
| Supply ↓ | ↑ | ↓ |
| Both ↑ | ? (depends on size) | ↑ |
| Demand ↑, supply ↓ | ↑ | ? |

:::ex Global markets in action
In 2022, Russia's invasion of Ukraine cut the supply of wheat, sunflower oil and natural gas: prices jumped and quantities fell. In the same years, falling battery costs and subsidies increased the supply of electric vehicles, lowering prices and raising sales.
:::

:::tip
Some markets clear in minutes (shares, currencies), others in years (housing, where supply takes time to build). The speed of adjustment is a useful evaluation point.
:::

### price-mechanism | The functions of the price mechanism
@terms
Signalling function :: Prices transmit information: rising prices tell producers that consumers want more of a good; falling prices that they want less.
Incentive function :: Higher prices motivate firms to produce more (higher profits) and lower prices motivate consumers to buy more.
Rationing function :: Prices ration scarce goods to those willing and able to pay the most.
Resource allocation :: How scarce factors of production are distributed between different uses.
@end
Adam Smith's **invisible hand**: prices coordinate millions of decisions without anyone planning them.

| Function | What happens when demand for honey rises |
| **Signalling** | The rising price tells beekeepers and potential entrants that consumers want more honey |
| **Incentive** | Higher profits encourage producers to keep more hives and new firms to enter |
| **Rationing** | The higher price rations the limited honey to those who value it most |

Resources move **out** of markets with falling prices and **into** markets with rising prices: this is **resource allocation** through the price mechanism.

:::tip
One way to keep the functions apart: a **shift** in a curve sends the **signal**; the resulting **movement along** the other curve is the **incentive** response; the new price **rations** the good.
:::

### surplus | Consumer surplus, producer surplus and allocative efficiency
@terms
Consumer surplus :: The difference between the highest price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay; the area below the demand curve and above the price.
Producer surplus :: The difference between the price producers receive and the lowest price they would accept; the area above the supply curve and below the price.
Social surplus :: Consumer surplus plus producer surplus (also called community surplus); maximised at the competitive equilibrium when there is no market failure.
Marginal benefit (MB) :: The extra benefit from consuming one more unit; shown by the demand curve.
Welfare loss :: A loss of social surplus caused by producing more or less than the allocatively efficient output (also called deadweight loss).
@end
{{dia:surplus}}
- The demand curve shows **marginal benefit** (what consumers would pay for each extra unit). The supply curve shows **marginal cost**.
- At equilibrium **MB = MC**: every unit worth more to consumers than it costs to produce is made, and none worth less. Social surplus is maximised: **allocative efficiency**.
- Any output other than Qe loses some surplus: a **welfare loss**.

When supply or demand increases, both CS and PS typically increase; government interventions such as taxes, subsidies and price controls redistribute surplus and create welfare losses ([[t2-7|2.7]]).

:::calc Measuring surplus from a diagram
Demand meets the price axis at $20, supply at $4, equilibrium P = $12, Q = 400.
CS = ½ × (20 − 12) × 400 = **$1,600**. PS = ½ × (12 − 4) × 400 = **$1,600**. Social surplus = **$3,200**.
:::

### eq-hl | Equilibrium and surplus from linear functions | HL
@terms
Market-clearing price :: The price at which Qd = Qs; found by setting the demand and supply functions equal.
@end
To find equilibrium, set **Qd = Qs** and solve for P, then substitute back for Q.

:::calc Equilibrium from functions
Qd = 120 − 4P and Qs = −30 + 6P.
120 − 4P = −30 + 6P → 150 = 10P → **P = $15**. Q = 120 − 4(15) = **60 units**.
Excess demand at P = $10: Qd = 80, Qs = 30 → **shortage of 50**.
Consumer surplus: demand meets the price axis where 120 − 4P = 0, P = $30. CS = ½ × (30 − 15) × 60 = **$450**.
Producer surplus: supply meets the price axis where −30 + 6P = 0, P = $5. PS = ½ × (15 − 5) × 60 = **$300**.
:::

:::tip
Check your answer by substituting P into **both** functions; they must give the same Q. Keep two decimal places in intermediate steps on Paper 3.
:::

## 2.4 | Critique of the maximizing behaviour of consumers and producers | SL | Rational choice and its limits, behavioural economics and nudges, and the objectives firms really pursue.

### rational-choice | Rational consumer choice and its limits
@terms
Rational consumer choice :: The assumption that consumers have perfect information, consistent preferences and choose so as to maximise their utility.
Utility :: The satisfaction a consumer gains from consuming a good or service.
Bounded rationality :: The idea that people make decisions that are "good enough" because they have limited information, time and mental capacity.
Bounded self-control :: People's limited willpower, which makes them act against their own long-term interests (e.g. overeating, undersaving).
Bounded selfishness :: The idea that people care about others and fairness, not only about themselves (e.g. charity, volunteering).
Imperfect information :: When buyers or sellers lack full knowledge of prices, quality or consequences, so they cannot make fully informed choices.
Biases :: Systematic errors in decision-making, such as rules of thumb, anchoring and framing, availability and loss aversion.
@end
Standard theory assumes consumers are **rational**, **maximise utility** and have **perfect information**. Behavioural economics shows these assumptions often fail.

| Limitation | What it means | Example |
| **Rule of thumb** | Shortcuts instead of careful calculation | Always buying the same brand; "spend 10% on rent…" |
| **Anchoring and framing** | Decisions depend on a reference point or on how options are presented | "Was $99, now $49"; "90% fat free" vs "10% fat" |
| **Availability bias** | Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind | Fear of flying after a news story about a crash |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel worse than equal gains feel good | Holding on to falling shares |
| **Bounded rationality** | Limited time and information, so people "satisfice" | Choosing a phone plan without comparing all 50 options |
| **Bounded self-control** | Limited willpower | Gym memberships unused; undersaving for retirement |
| **Bounded selfishness** | People also care about others | Donations, fair-trade purchases |
| **Imperfect information** | Consumers cannot judge quality or consequences | Health risks of vaping; quality of a used car |

:::tip
These limitations are ready-made evaluation for any question that assumes rational behaviour, e.g. "a tax will reduce consumption" (addiction and bounded self-control weaken the effect).
:::

### behavioural | Behavioural economics: choice architecture and nudges
@terms
Choice architecture :: The design of the way choices are presented to people, which influences the decisions they make.
Default choice :: An option that applies automatically unless the person actively chooses otherwise.
Restricted choice :: Limiting the number of options so people are not overwhelmed (e.g. a limited set of pension funds).
Mandated choice :: Requiring people to make an active decision (e.g. stating whether they want to be an organ donor when applying for a licence).
Nudge theory :: The idea that small, low-cost changes in how choices are presented can steer people towards better decisions without removing any option or changing incentives much.
@end
#### Choice architecture
| Type | How it works | Example |
| **Default** | The preferred option is pre-selected; most people never change it | UK workplace pensions auto-enrolment (opt-out); opt-out organ donation in Spain, Wales, England |
| **Restricted** | Fewer, simpler options reduce confusion | Sweden's pension system limits the menu of funds |
| **Mandated** | People must actively choose | Organ donor question on a driving licence form in many US states |

#### Nudges
- Placing fruit at eye level and sweets out of reach in school canteens.
- Sending letters saying "9 out of 10 people in your town pay their tax on time" (UK tax authority's Behavioural Insights Team raised payment rates).
- Graphic warnings and plain packaging on cigarettes.
- Showing households how their energy use compares with neighbours'.
- Painted flies in airport urinals (Amsterdam Schiphol) to reduce spillage.

:::eval Advantages | Disadvantages
+ Cheap compared with taxes, subsidies or regulation.
+ Preserve freedom of choice: nothing is banned.
+ Can be very effective at scale (pension auto-enrolment raised participation from around 60% to over 85% in the UK).
- Effects can be small or fade over time.
- Ethical concerns: manipulation; who decides what is "good"?
- Firms also use nudges against consumers' interests (dark patterns, default subscriptions).
- Hard to measure effects; works better for some groups than others.
:::

### business-objectives | Business objectives
@terms
Profit maximisation :: Producing the output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC), so total revenue minus total cost is as large as possible.
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) :: A firm acting ethically and in the interests of society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
Market share :: A firm's sales as a percentage of total sales in its market.
Satisficing :: Aiming for a satisfactory level of profit rather than the maximum, often to balance the interests of different stakeholders.
Revenue maximisation :: Producing where marginal revenue is zero, so total revenue is as large as possible.
Growth maximisation :: Aiming to make the firm as large as possible, measured by sales, output, market share or assets.
@end
Standard theory assumes firms **maximise profit**. In reality they pursue several objectives:

| Objective | Why a firm pursues it | Evaluation |
| **Profit maximisation** (MR = MC) | Rewards shareholders; funds investment and R&D | Firms rarely know their exact MR and MC; may harm reputation or stakeholders |
| **Corporate social responsibility** | Brand image, customer loyalty, attracting staff and investors, ethical owners | Higher costs; risk of "greenwashing" (e.g. Volkswagen's emissions scandal 2015) |
| **Market share / growth** | Economies of scale, market power, security against takeover | May involve low prices and lower profit in the short run (e.g. Amazon's early years) |
| **Revenue maximisation** (MR = 0) | Managers' pay or status linked to sales | Output higher and price lower than with profit maximisation |
| **Satisficing** | Separation of ownership and control: managers balance stakeholders; profit "good enough" | Common in large firms and family businesses; less efficient? |

:::ex CSR in practice
Patagonia gives 1% of sales to environmental causes and in 2022 transferred ownership to a trust so that profits fund climate action. Unilever and Ben & Jerry's pursue social objectives alongside profit.
:::

## 2.5 | Elasticities of demand | SL | PED and YED: calculation, interpretation, determinants, and why they matter for firms, governments and development.

### ped | Price elasticity of demand (PED)
@terms
Price elasticity of demand (PED) :: A measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price: %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP.
Price elastic demand :: When the percentage change in quantity demanded is greater than the percentage change in price (|PED| > 1).
Price inelastic demand :: When the percentage change in quantity demanded is less than the percentage change in price (|PED| < 1).
Unit elastic demand :: When the percentage change in quantity demanded equals the percentage change in price (|PED| = 1).
Perfectly inelastic demand :: When quantity demanded does not change at all as price changes (PED = 0).
Perfectly elastic demand :: When any rise in price causes quantity demanded to fall to zero (PED = ∞).
@end
:::formula PED
PED = \frac{%ΔQd}{%ΔP} :: always negative for normal demand curves; interpret using the absolute value
%Δ = \frac{new − old}{old} × 100
:::
:::calc PED
A firm raises its price from $10 to $15 and sales fall from 100 to 40 a day.
%ΔQd = (40 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = −60%. %ΔP = (15 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = +50%.
PED = −60 ÷ 50 = **−1.2** → price elastic: the % fall in sales is larger than the % rise in price.
Rearranging: if PED = −0.4 and price rises 10%, %ΔQd = −0.4 × 10 = **−4%**.
:::

| Value (absolute) | Description | Example |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Insulin for diabetics (approximately) |
| 0 – 1 | Inelastic | Petrol, cigarettes, salt, electricity |
| 1 | Unit elastic | |
| 1 – ∞ | Elastic | A particular brand of jeans, restaurant meals, holidays |
| ∞ | Perfectly elastic | One farmer's wheat in a world market |

{{dia:ped-compare}}
{{dia:ped-special}}

#### Determinants of PED
- **Substitutes**: more and closer substitutes → more elastic (one brand of coffee vs coffee in general).
- **Proportion of income**: goods that take a big share of income → more elastic (cars vs matches).
- **Necessity vs luxury**: necessities are inelastic.
- **Addictiveness / habit**: addictive goods are inelastic (tobacco, alcohol).
- **Time**: demand becomes more elastic over time as consumers find alternatives (petrol: switching to EVs or public transport takes years).
- **Breadth of definition**: "food" is inelastic; "Heinz baked beans" is elastic.

:::tip
State the negative sign in your calculation, then interpret using the absolute value. Never write PED as a percentage.
:::

### ped-significance | Significance of PED: revenue, firms, governments and commodities
@terms
Total revenue (TR) :: The total income a firm receives from sales: price × quantity sold.
Primary commodities :: Raw materials and agricultural goods, such as oil, copper, coffee and wheat.
Tax incidence :: How the burden of a tax is shared between consumers and producers.
@end
#### PED and total revenue
{{dia:ped-tr}}
| PED | Price rise | Price cut |
| Inelastic (< 1) | TR **rises** | TR falls |
| Elastic (> 1) | TR falls | TR **rises** |
| Unit elastic | TR unchanged | TR unchanged |

:::hl PED varies along a straight-line demand curve
{{dia:ped-linear}}
On a linear demand curve the slope is constant, but PED = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q) changes because P ÷ Q changes. Demand is elastic at high prices, unit elastic at the midpoint and inelastic at low prices. **TR is maximised at the midpoint**, where PED = 1 and MR = 0. So a single straight-line demand curve does not have "a" PED.
:::

#### Why PED matters
- **Firms**: pricing decisions. Firms facing inelastic demand (with market power) can raise prices and revenue; those facing elastic demand compete on price. Airlines charge business travellers (inelastic) more than leisure travellers.
- **Governments**: tax revenue and tax incidence. Indirect taxes on goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco, alcohol) raise a lot of revenue but reduce consumption only a little, and most of the tax is passed on to consumers ([[e-indirect-taxes|2.7]]).
- **Primary commodities vs manufactured goods**: primary commodities generally have **low PED** (few substitutes, necessities), so a small change in supply causes a big change in price. Combined with low PES, this makes commodity prices volatile and farmers' and commodity exporters' incomes unstable (a key development issue, [[t4-9|4.9]]). Manufactured goods have more substitutes and more elastic demand.

:::ex Coffee price swings
Frost and drought in Brazil in 2021 cut coffee supply; with inelastic demand, arabica prices roughly doubled within a year. Producer countries such as Ethiopia and Uganda, which rely on coffee exports, see export revenue swing sharply.
:::

### yed | Income elasticity of demand (YED)
@terms
Income elasticity of demand (YED) :: A measure of the responsiveness of demand for a good to a change in consumers' income: %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY.
Income elastic (luxury) good :: A normal good with YED > 1: demand rises more than proportionately with income.
Income inelastic (necessity) good :: A normal good with YED between 0 and 1.
Sectoral change :: The shift of an economy's output and employment from the primary sector to the secondary and then the tertiary sector as incomes rise.
@end
:::formula YED
YED = \frac{%ΔQd}{%ΔY} :: the sign matters: positive = normal, negative = inferior
:::
:::calc YED
Weekly income rises from S$100 to S$125 (+25%) and bubble tea purchases rise from 12 to 15 (+25%). YED = 25 ÷ 25 = **+1** (normal good, unit income elastic).
:::
| YED | Type of good | Examples |
| > 1 | Normal, income elastic (luxury) | Foreign holidays, sports cars, fine dining |
| 0 – 1 | Normal, income inelastic (necessity) | Bread, toothpaste, electricity |
| < 0 | Inferior | Bus travel, instant noodles, second-hand clothes |

#### Why YED matters
- **Firms**: in a boom, demand for luxury goods grows fastest; in a recession, discount retailers (inferior goods) may gain. Firms diversify product ranges to manage this risk.
- **Sectoral change**: primary products (food) have low YED, manufactured goods higher, services highest. As economies grow, spending and employment move from agriculture to manufacturing to services. Countries exporting primary commodities gain less from world income growth than exporters of manufactured goods and services: a reason for developing economies to **diversify**.

:::tip
For YED the sign is essential: always state whether the good is normal or inferior.
:::

:::key Cross elasticity (XED)
XED (%ΔQd of A ÷ %ΔP of B) was removed from the current guide, but the idea of substitutes (positive) and complements (negative) still appears in the determinants of demand. You will not be asked to calculate XED.
:::

## 2.6 | Elasticity of supply | SL | PES: calculation, determinants, and why commodity supply is so inelastic.

### pes | Price elasticity of supply (PES)
@terms
Price elasticity of supply (PES) :: A measure of the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the good's price: %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP.
Price elastic supply :: When the percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price (PES > 1).
Price inelastic supply :: When the percentage change in quantity supplied is less than the percentage change in price (PES < 1).
@end
:::formula PES
PES = \frac{%ΔQs}{%ΔP} :: positive, because supply curves slope upward
:::
:::calc PES
Avocado prices rise from AU$0.90 to AU$1.45 (+61.11%). A farm shop raises its weekly supply from 110 to 120 (+9.09%). PES = 9.09 ÷ 61.11 = **0.15** → very inelastic: the farm cannot grow more avocados quickly.
:::
{{dia:pes-range}}
#### Determinants of PES
- **Time period**: the most important. Momentary (fixed), short run (only variable factors change), long run (all factors can change). PES rises over time.
- **Spare capacity**: firms with idle machines and workers can raise output quickly.
- **Stocks**: firms with inventories can supply more immediately; perishables cannot be stored.
- **Mobility of factors**: if resources can be switched easily between uses, supply is more elastic.
- **Complexity of production / rate of cost increase**: if costs rise sharply as output expands, supply is inelastic.

#### Primary commodities vs manufactured goods
Agricultural goods take a growing season to respond (coffee bushes take 3–4 years to mature; a new copper mine 10+ years), so their supply is **price inelastic**. Manufactured goods can usually be scaled up by adding shifts or using spare capacity, so supply is **more elastic**. Inelastic supply plus inelastic demand → **highly volatile commodity prices**.

:::tip
Think like a producer: PES is about how easily firms can change output, not how consumers react. PES is never expressed as a percentage.
:::

## 2.7 | Role of government in microeconomics | SL | Why governments intervene and how: indirect taxes, subsidies, price controls, direct provision, regulation and nudges, with HL welfare calculations.

### why-intervene | Reasons for government intervention
@terms
Market failure :: When the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently, so that the socially optimal output is not produced.
Stakeholder :: Any person or group affected by a decision, e.g. consumers, producers, workers, the government, the wider community.
@end
Governments intervene in markets to:
1. **Earn revenue** (indirect taxes on goods with inelastic demand).
2. **Support firms** (subsidies to infant industries, farmers or strategic sectors).
3. **Support low-income households** (price ceilings on necessities, subsidies on food and fuel, minimum wages).
4. **Influence production and consumption** (discourage demerit goods, encourage merit goods).
5. **Correct market failure** (externalities, public goods, asymmetric information, market power: [[t2-8|2.8]]–[[t2-11|2.11]]).
6. **Promote equity** ([[t2-12|2.12]]).

The tools: **indirect taxes, subsidies, price controls, direct provision, regulation and legislation, and consumer nudges**. For each, analyse effects on **stakeholders**: consumers, producers, workers, the government and society.

### indirect-taxes | Indirect taxes
@terms
Indirect tax :: A tax on spending on goods and services, paid to the government by producers, who pass some of it on to consumers.
Specific tax :: A fixed amount of tax per unit of a good, e.g. $1 per litre.
Ad valorem tax :: A tax that is a percentage of the price of a good, e.g. 20% VAT.
Excise tax :: An indirect tax on a specific good such as tobacco, alcohol or fuel.
Consumer burden :: The part of an indirect tax paid by consumers through higher prices.
Producer burden :: The part of an indirect tax paid by producers through a lower price received.
@end
{{dia:tax-specific}}
{{dia:tax-adval}}
#### Effects on stakeholders
- **Consumers**: pay a higher price (Pc), buy less; lose consumer surplus.
- **Producers**: receive a lower price (Pp), sell less; lose producer surplus; may cut jobs.
- **Government**: gains revenue (Pc − Pp) × Q₂, which may fund public services.
- **Society**: a welfare loss if there was no externality; a welfare **gain** if the tax corrects a negative externality ([[e-externality-policy|2.8]]).

:::hl Tax incidence, PED and welfare calculations
{{dia:tax-incidence}}
The burden falls mostly on whichever side of the market is **less elastic**. With inelastic demand (petrol, tobacco), consumers pay most of the tax and quantity falls only a little: good for revenue, weak for changing behaviour.
**Calculating from a diagram**: P₁ = $10, Q₁ = 500; tax = $4; after the tax Pc = $13, Pp = $9, Q₂ = 400.
- Tax revenue = 4 × 400 = **$1,600**
- Consumer burden = (13 − 10) × 400 = **$1,200**; producer burden = (10 − 9) × 400 = **$400**
- Welfare loss = ½ × 4 × (500 − 400) = **$200**
- Change in CS = −[(13 − 10) × 400 + ½ × 3 × 100] = **−$1,350**
:::

:::eval Advantages | Disadvantages
+ Raise revenue for public spending.
+ Discourage consumption of harmful goods; can correct negative externalities.
+ Market-based: consumers still choose.
- Regressive: low-income households spend a larger share of income on taxed goods.
- If demand is inelastic, consumption falls only slightly.
- Encourage smuggling and black markets if very high (e.g. tobacco in Australia).
- Hard to set the "right" rate; political unpopularity (France's "gilets jaunes" protests against fuel tax rises, 2018).
:::

### subsidies | Subsidies
@terms
Subsidy :: A payment by the government to producers (or consumers) per unit, to lower costs and prices and increase output and consumption.
@end
{{dia:subsidy}}
Governments subsidise to make necessities affordable (food, fuel), support farmers and strategic industries, encourage merit goods and positive externalities (education, renewable energy, EVs), and help domestic firms compete internationally.

#### Effects on stakeholders
- **Consumers**: lower price (Pc), consume more.
- **Producers**: receive more per unit (Pp), produce more; incomes rise.
- **Workers**: more jobs in the subsidised industry.
- **Government**: pays (Pp − Pc) × Q₂; opportunity cost of that spending.
- **Foreign producers**: face unfair competition (subsidised exports can be dumped).
- **Society**: welfare loss from overallocation if there is no positive externality.

:::hl Subsidy calculation
P₁ = $20, Q₁ = 1,000; a subsidy of $6 per unit; new Pc = $17, Pp = $23, Q₂ = 1,300.
- Government spending = subsidy × Q₂ = 6 × 1,300 = **$7,800**
- Gain in consumer surplus = trapezium between P₁ and Pc = (20 − 17) × (1,000 + 1,300) ÷ 2 = **$3,450**
- Gain in producer surplus = trapezium between Pp and P₁ = (23 − 20) × (1,000 + 1,300) ÷ 2 = **$3,450**
- Welfare loss = ½ × subsidy × (Q₂ − Q₁) = ½ × 6 × 300 = **$900**
- Check: spending = ΔCS + ΔPS + welfare loss → 7,800 = 3,450 + 3,450 + 900 ✓
:::

:::eval Advantages | Disadvantages
+ Lower prices for consumers; helps low-income households.
+ Raise output and employment; support strategic industries.
+ Correct positive externalities (vaccines, renewable energy).
- Opportunity cost for government budgets.
- Firms may become inefficient and dependent; hard to remove (fuel subsidies in Nigeria, ended 2023 amid protests).
- Overproduction and dumping (EU and US farm subsidies hurt farmers in developing countries).
- Hard to target and to measure the right size.
:::

### price-ceilings | Price ceilings (maximum prices)
@terms
Price ceiling :: A legal maximum price set by the government below the equilibrium price.
Parallel market :: An illegal (black) market where goods are sold above the legal maximum price.
Non-price rationing :: Allocating goods by methods other than price, such as queues, lotteries, coupons or favouritism.
@end
{{dia:price-ceiling}}
Used for necessities (food staples, rent, fuel) to make them affordable. Examples: rent controls in Berlin (2020, later struck down), Stockholm and San Francisco; bread and fuel price caps in Egypt, Venezuela, Hungary (2021–23 on fuel and food).

#### Consequences
- **Shortage** (Qd − Qs): queues, waiting lists, first come first served, rationing.
- **Parallel markets**: goods resold at Pb (the black-market price).
- **Lower quality** and less maintenance (landlords neglect rent-controlled housing).
- **Underallocation of resources** and welfare loss.

:::hl Welfare effects of a price ceiling
Consumers who still buy gain the rectangle (Pe − Pmax) × Qs from producers; consumers lose the triangle of units no longer sold. Producer surplus falls by the rectangle plus its own triangle. The two triangles between Qs and Qe are the **welfare loss**.
Example: Pe = $8, Qe = 600; Pmax = $5, Qs = 450, Qd = 750; the demand curve at Qs = 450 shows willingness to pay of $11. Shortage = **300**. Welfare loss = ½ × (11 − 5) × (600 − 450) = **$450**.
:::

:::eval For | Against
+ Makes necessities affordable for low-income consumers who can buy.
+ Can be justified in emergencies (festive periods, wartime).
- Shortages: some consumers get nothing.
- Black markets, lower quality, less investment (fewer new rental homes built).
- Firms may leave the market; government may need to subsidise or supply directly.
- Long-run supply is more elastic, so shortages grow over time.
:::

### price-floors | Price floors (minimum prices) and minimum wages
@terms
Price floor :: A legal minimum price set above the equilibrium price.
Minimum wage :: A legal minimum hourly wage that employers must pay workers, a price floor in the labour market.
Buffer stock :: A stock of a commodity bought by an authority when prices are low and sold when prices are high, to stabilise prices.
@end
{{dia:price-floor}}
Price floors protect producers' incomes (agriculture), or discourage consumption (minimum unit pricing for alcohol in Scotland, 2018, at 50p then 65p per unit).

#### Consequences
- **Surplus** (Qs − Qd). The government may buy it (cost Pmin × surplus), store it, destroy it, export it (dumping), or pay farmers to produce less.
- **Consumers** pay more and buy less; **efficient producers** earn more; **inefficient producers** are protected; **taxpayers** fund the purchases (EU "butter mountains" and "wine lakes" in the 1980s).
- **Overallocation** of resources and welfare loss.

:::hl Welfare and cost of a price floor
Example: Pe = $4, Qe = 1,000; Pmin = $6, Qd = 800, Qs = 1,400. Surplus = **600 units**. If the government buys the surplus: cost = 6 × 600 = **$3,600**. Consumer surplus falls by (6 − 4) × 800 plus the small triangle; producers gain the rectangle and extra sales. The welfare loss includes the cost of producing units no one consumes.
:::

#### Minimum wages
{{dia:min-wage}}
:::eval Benefits | Costs
+ Raises incomes of low-paid workers; reduces poverty and in-work benefit spending.
+ May raise productivity and morale (efficiency wages); reduces exploitation.
+ In monopsony labour markets, can raise wages and employment.
- Unemployment if Wmin is well above equilibrium, especially for young and unskilled workers.
- Higher costs may be passed on as higher prices; firms may automate.
- Encourages informal (black-market) employment in developing countries.
- Evidence is mixed: the UK National Living Wage has caused little measurable job loss so far.
:::

### direct-provision | Direct provision, regulation and nudges
@terms
Direct provision :: When the government itself supplies a good or service, funded through taxation, e.g. state schools, hospitals, roads.
Regulation :: Rules and laws set by government to control the behaviour of firms or consumers, e.g. bans, standards, licences.
@end
| Tool | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| **Direct provision** | State schools and hospitals, street lighting, national defence, public housing | Ensures access for all, especially low-income groups; corrects underprovision of merit and public goods | High opportunity cost; can be inefficient without competition; quality may suffer |
| **Regulation and legislation** | Bans on smoking indoors, age limits on alcohol, emissions standards, food safety laws, plastic bag bans | Clear and direct; easy to understand | Enforcement and monitoring costs; black markets; firms may lobby or evade |
| **Consumer nudges** | Default options, calorie labels, graphic warnings | Cheap; keep freedom of choice | Effects uncertain and may be small ([[e-behavioural|2.4]]) |

:::tip
In any intervention essay, compare at least two tools and name the **stakeholders** who gain and lose. Conclude with a judgement that depends on the **context**: PED, how big the external cost is, the government's budget, and the risk of government failure.
:::
